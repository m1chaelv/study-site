S1 · Legal System & Courts
LO 1Define the basic structure of the U.S. legal system and its relationship to cybersecurity issues▼

Who makes, enforces, and interprets law — and which court a cyber dispute travels through.

  • Constitutional structure — Article I: Congress and the power to create federal law; Article II: executive authority and enforcement; Article III: the federal judiciary; Article VI: supremacy of federal law when federal and state rules intersect.
  • Three-tier federal courts — 94 district courts (trial courts of limited jurisdiction), 12 regional circuit courts of appeals plus the Federal Circuit (three-judge panels, no new evidence), and the Supreme Court (nine justices, discretionary review by writ of certiorari, about 70–80 cases a term).
  • Federal vs. state jurisdiction — state courts have general jurisdiction; federal courts hear federal-law, constitutional, state-vs-state, and diversity cases above a threshold; patent and bankruptcy are exclusively federal.
  • Texas’s bifurcated top — the Supreme Court of Texas (civil and juvenile) and the Court of Criminal Appeals (criminal); 15 intermediate courts of appeals including the Fifteenth (created 2024, hears Business Court appeals); district, county, justice, and municipal courts below.
  • Cyber cases in Texas courts — felony cybercrimes under Texas Penal Code Chapter 33 go to district courts; misdemeanor online offenses may start at county or municipal level, where courts weigh authenticity, chain of custody, and lawful collection.
  • Path to the Supreme Court — cybercrime cases (e.g., CFAA) usually start in U.S. district court, go to a court of appeals, then a cert petition; the Court takes cases with constitutional questions, conflicting statutory interpretations, circuit splits, or national impact.
S2 · Law, Code & Lawmaking
LO 2Explain how cyber law applies traditional legal concepts to technology-related problems▼

Old legal authority, new technology: code regulates behavior, automated systems enforce rules, and legislatures respond to incidents.

  • Cyber law borrows from everything — constitutional law, criminal law, privacy law, contracts, intellectual property, regulatory authority, and evidence; it is not a single isolated subject.
  • “Code is law” (Lessig) — software architecture can regulate behavior like legal rules: code, platform design, access controls, automated permissions, protocols, and technical enforcement shape conduct alongside statutes and courts. Blockchain is the course’s guiding example.
  • Automated rule enforcement — the automated ball-strike system raises questions about accuracy, transparency, appeals, trust, human judgment, and institutional authority, the same questions social-media moderation raises.
  • How a need becomes a law — trigger event and problem definition → agenda-setting and stakeholder mobilization → research and policy options → drafting and sponsorship → committee hearings and markup → floor debate → second chamber and reconciliation → executive action → rulemaking and implementation → oversight and amendment.
  • The breach-notice example — the module’s Texas school ransomware story shows a gap (no clear rule for fast disclosure) producing policy options: define “breach” and “personal information,” set notification deadlines, create penalties, and provide safe harbors such as encryption exceptions.
  • Law lags technology — supplemental readings debate whether cyberlaw needs its own categories (Easterbrook’s “Law of the Horse”) and how technology spreads before lawmakers respond, which is why organizations need strong internal policies.
S3 · Legal Reasoning & Research
LO 3Apply IRAC-style legal reasoning to basic cyber law and policy questions▼

Turn a messy fact pattern into Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion — then find and cite the authority.

  • Fact pattern — a summary of the key facts (who, what, when, where, how, why) without discussing the legal consequences; it is the foundation for analysis.
  • IRAC — Issue (the legal question), Rule (the governing law), Application (apply the rule to the specific facts, not just summarize), Conclusion (the supported answer). Avoid jumping to an unsupported conclusion.
  • Nested IRAC — when a secondary legal issue needs its own question, rule, analysis, and conclusion inside the main analysis; it keeps complex arguments organized so no element is missed.
  • Worked example (negligence) — the Jane Smith charity 5K: duty, breach, causation, and damages applied to volunteers who ignored a reported banana peel for about five minutes.
  • Citation format — Case Name, Volume Reporter Page (Court Year), e.g., Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967); statutes as Law Name, Title U.S.C. § Section (Year); regulations as Title C.F.R. § Section; “U.S.” = Supreme Court reporter, “F.3d” = federal appeals reporter.
  • Dorking as legal OSINT — operators site:, filetype:, intitle:, inurl:, and quotation marks find indexed public sources; searching is not permission — public visibility is not authorization to access, download, test, or reuse.
S4 · Policy Risk & Gaps
LO 4Analyze a broken data policy for control, compliance, and audit gaps▼

Read like a reviewer: what does Northstar say, what does it forget, and what would it need to prove later?

  • A gap is something important that is missing, weak, unclear, or not supported by evidence.
  • Control gap — the policy doesn’t clearly explain how Northstar will prevent, detect, or reduce a security or privacy problem (e.g., secure access with no mention of MFA, role-based access, or access reviews).
  • Compliance gap — the policy doesn’t clearly explain how Northstar will meet a legal, regulatory, contractual, or privacy responsibility (e.g., no plan for EU/Ireland privacy expectations).
  • Audit gap — the policy makes a promise but doesn’t say what evidence would prove it (e.g., backups “tested” with no frequency, owner, results location, or success criteria).
  • Reviewer questions — What does Northstar say it will do? Is it clear? Is there a control behind it? Is there a legal or compliance expectation behind it? Could Northstar prove it did it?
  • Sources by gap type — control: NIST CSF 2.0 and CISA Cybersecurity Performance Goals; compliance: GDPR and the NIS2 Directive; audit: NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 and SP 800-53A Rev. 5.
LO 5Evaluate initial organizational risk based on policy weaknesses and missing governance language▼

Score it, treat it, and name the proof: the Project 1 method.

  • Scoring — likelihood (1 rare – 5 almost certain) × impact (1 minimal – 5 severe) = risk score; ratings: 1–4 Low, 5–9 Medium, 10–16 High, 17–25 Critical.
  • Four treatments — accept (document who approved, why, and when it will be reviewed), avoid (stop or change the activity), transfer (contracts, insurance, vendor agreements, SLAs, outsourcing), mitigate/reduce (keep the activity but add safeguards such as MFA, encryption, training, access reviews, logging, monitoring).
  • Risk categories — technical, legal, privacy, operational, governance, vendor, financial, reputational, employee/HR, physical security, or international compliance.
  • Nine-part format — policy statement, risk explanation, category, likelihood, impact, score and rating, treatment, recommended change, evidence needed.
  • Evidence needed — proof the change happened: access review records, MFA reports, encryption standards, training records, vendor reviews, incident documentation, backup test results, legal review notes, inventory updates, screenshots, audit reports, signed approvals, or risk-acceptance forms.
  • Not every risk is technical — leadership decisions, privacy, vendors, legal compliance, employee behavior, incident response, retention, monitoring, public trust, and international operations all count.

Guiding question: why does understanding these articles matter before studying cybercrime, privacy, and cybersecurity regulation? Because cyber law questions often turn on who has authority to regulate, investigate, enforce, or interpret obligations.

The federal judiciary

One of three coequal branches in a system of checks and balances. Congress establishes federal courts; the President appoints judges; the Senate confirms them. Judges interpret and apply federal, constitutional, and international law.

TierRoleKey facts
U.S. district courtsGeneral federal trial courts — hearings, evidence, motions, verdicts94 districts; civil and criminal; each has a bankruptcy unit (Article I, 14-year judges); magistrate judges serve 8-year (full-time) or 4-year (part-time) renewable terms; district judges have lifetime Article III appointments
U.S. courts of appealsReview district courts and some agencies for legal and procedural error12 regional circuits + the Federal Circuit; three-judge panels; no witnesses or new evidence
U.S. Supreme CourtFinal arbiter of federal and constitutional questionsNine justices (Chief + 8 Associate), lifetime terms; discretionary review by writ of certiorari; about 1% of petitions, typically 70–80 cases a term; decisions bind all lower federal and state courts
  • Specialized courts — Court of International Trade (New York; tariffs, import restrictions, duties); Court of Federal Claims (Article I; money claims against the U.S. — contracts, Fifth Amendment takings, tax refunds), reviewed by the Federal Circuit.
  • Federal Circuit — nationwide, specialized: patents, international trade, claims against the government; uniformity in technical areas.
  • Jurisdiction — state courts are general jurisdiction; U.S. district courts are limited to cases the Constitution and statutes authorize: federal law, constitutional rights, disputes between states, and diversity cases above a threshold. Patent and bankruptcy are exclusively federal.
  • Support structures — the Judicial Conference (policymaking), the Administrative Office (budget, personnel, technology), the Federal Judicial Center (est. 1967; education and research), circuit judicial councils, and the U.S. Marshals Service (security).
  • Transparency — most proceedings are public; records via clerks’ offices and PACER. Texas: the Texas Public Information Act (Gov’t Code § 552) and re:SearchTX, with limits for juvenile, sensitive family, trade-secret, and national-security matters.

Digital evidence in every court — courts decide authenticity, chain of custody, and whether records were lawfully obtained. Screenshots can be edited, accounts shared, timestamps misread. Forensic work (bit-for-bit images, hash values, deleted-file recovery, log correlation) must stay within warrant scope and be explainable in plain language. The module’s repeatable questions: What court? Criminal, civil, regulatory, or contractual? What evidence proves the facts? Who collected it? Was collection lawful? What privacy interests? What remedy?

Pathways to the Supreme Court for cyber cases

Cybercrime cases (e.g., CFAA) usually start in U.S. district court → court of appeals → petition for certiorari. The module lists scenarios that could reach the Court:

  • Constitutional challenges — e.g., Fourth Amendment limits on accessing encrypted or cloud-stored data without a warrant.
  • Statutory interpretation — conflicting readings of how far the CFAA reaches.
  • Circuit splits — appellate courts disagree on the same cyber question.
  • National impact — sweeping effects on civil liberties or commerce.
  • Free speech — limits of online expression and platform moderation.
📌 The module lists the justices “as of 2024” (Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett, Jackson). The scenario list is credited in the source to “OpenAI, 2025.”
🧩 Study aid — why this keeps coming back. Module 02’s cases follow this exact path: Nosal I and II were Ninth Circuit decisions (a regional court of appeals), and Van Buren reached the Supreme Court because circuits disagreed about how broadly the CFAA reads “exceeds authorized access.”

How a need becomes a law (cybercrime example)

The module’s Schoolhouse Rock–inspired story: a Texas middle school is hit by ransomware; systems go down, student data may be stolen, staff aren’t sure whether FERPA applies, and families wait weeks for answers. The gap — no clear rule requiring fast disclosure — becomes the trigger for the Texas Data Breach Notification Act.

🧠 S3 · Legal Reasoning, Citation & Research▼

Fact patterns

A fact pattern summarizes the key facts — who did what, when, where, how, and why — without discussing legal consequences. A good analysis shows a clear presentation of facts, a systematic analysis of legal elements, application of law to facts, and potential outcomes. The module’s examples: John Doe and the armed burglar (self-defense and Texas’s castle doctrine) and Tony Soprano’s disappearance (declaring a missing person legally dead after a continuous absence).

IRAC

StepWhat it doesVan Buren (your IRAC)
IssueThe precise legal questionDoes an officer violate the CFAA by accessing a database he’s authorized to use, for personal gain against policy?
RuleThe governing law18 U.S.C. § 1030(a)(2) and § 1030(e)(6); exceeding authorized access = obtaining information from areas that are off-limits, not misusing information you may obtain
ApplicationConnect the rule to these facts — not a summaryHe used valid credentials to retrieve a category of information he was entitled to; his purpose was improper but the access wasn’t
ConclusionThe supported answerNo CFAA violation (6–3, 2021); the ruling doesn’t excuse the misconduct or bar other charges

Nested IRAC — an IRAC sequence inside a broader IRAC when a secondary issue needs its own question, rule, analysis, and conclusion. It organizes complex arguments and reduces the chance of missing an element. A good nested issue is distinct but related — for Van Buren, whether a department purpose restriction can define “authorization.”

Worked example: Jane Smith and the charity 5K

Jane slipped on a banana peel at the 2-mile mark; volunteers about 10 feet away were told but didn’t remove it, and it was visible about five minutes. She fractured her wrist, had a mild concussion, $8,500 in medical bills, and $6,000 in lost wages.

Negligence elementApplied
Duty of careThe organizer owed participants a reasonably safe course and a response to hazards
BreachVolunteers ignored a reported, visible hazard for about five minutes
CausationThe peel directly caused the fall; injury foreseeable after notice (proximate cause)
DamagesMedical expenses, lost wages, and lingering pain

Dorking is OSINT: it needs no break-in, only careful searching of what search engines have indexed. Defensively it finds accidental exposure — public login pages, old documents, directories, forgotten test pages, configuration references — before attackers do. Searching is not permission: public visibility doesn’t authorize opening, downloading, testing, reusing, or sharing results. Stay in scope, follow rules of engagement, and when in doubt, stop and ask.
