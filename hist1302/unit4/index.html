Learning Objectives — Prof. Walter's 24
1
Foreign events in 1930s Europe; the rise of Hitler & Mussolini; German imperialism & appeasement in Czechoslovakia; how WWII began with the invasion of Poland.
CH. 27
  • Benito Mussolini built Fascism (Fasci Italiani di Combattimento, 1919) and became Italy's PM in 1922
  • Adolf Hitler → chancellor Jan 1933 (appointed by Paul von Hindenburg) → Enabling Act made him dictator; Nuremberg Laws (1935)
  • Aggression: Rhineland (1936) → Austria (1938) → Munich Pact (1938, Neville Chamberlain + Édouard Daladier, Sudetenland, appeasement, "peace in our time")
  • WWII began with the Blitzkrieg on Poland, Sept 1, 1939; Britain & France declared war Sept 3
2
Why the U.S. declared neutrality at the onset of WWII.
CH. 27
  • Strong isolationist tradition and bitter memory of World War I
  • Senator Gerald P. Nye argued bankers and industrialists had tricked the U.S. into WWI
  • The Neutrality Acts (1935, 1936, 1937) banned arms sales, loans, and travel/transport to belligerents
3
WWII before U.S. involvement, 1939–1941, by location (Europe & Asia).
CH. 27
  • Europe: German Blitzkrieg overran Poland then France (1940); Battle of Britain; FDR aided Britain via Cash and Carry (1939) and Lend-Lease (1941)
  • Asia: Japan seized French Indochina → U.S. Export Control Act embargo (1940)
  • The Philippines (a U.S. territory) sat on Japan's oil route → rising tension → Pearl Harbor, Dec 7, 1941
4
WWII after the U.S. entered, by year and location.
CH. 27
  • Europe: 1942 greatest German extent, Allies land in N. Africa (vs. Erwin Rommel); 1943 bombing + fall of Mussolini; 1944 D-Day (June 6) + Paris freed; 1945 Berlin, Hitler's suicide, surrender May 8
  • Asia: Japan seized the Philippines; Douglas MacArthur; the navy turned the tide at Midway via island hopping
  • 1945 Pacific: Iwo Jima, Okinawa, sustained bombing of Japan
5
Truman's decision to drop the atomic bombs; the difficulties of demobilization; the "Fair Deal."
CH. 27 → 28
  • Harry S. Truman dropped the bombs to force surrender and avoid a home-island invasion (projected up to ~250,000 U.S. deaths); a possible motive was signaling the USSR
  • Demobilization was rushed — the military shrank from ~12 million to ~1.5 million by 1947
  • The Fair Deal (1949): higher minimum wage, Social Security expansion, public housing; Truman desegregated the armed forces by executive order (1948)
6
The term "Cold War" and the importance of the postwar conferences.
CH. 28
  • The Cold War = the U.S.–Soviet struggle for supremacy fought without direct combat (espionage, propaganda, arms race, proxy wars)
  • Yalta (1945): Churchill & Roosevelt made concessions strengthening Stalin in Eastern Europe
  • Potsdam (1945): the Allies finalized the four-zone division of Germany
7
Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe; why the USSR held it; why the wartime allies became adversaries.
CH. 28
  • Joseph Stalin installed Communist, pro-Soviet governments (e.g., Poland) to spread Communism and buffer the USSR against future invasion
  • The alliance collapsed over incompatible visions for postwar Europe — Soviet expansion/security vs. U.S. promotion of democracy and free markets
8
George Kennan's containment policy; the "Iron Curtain."
CH. 28
  • George Kennan's "Long Telegram" (1946): the USSR couldn't accept coexistence, so it must be contained, mainly through economic policy
  • Containment was the core U.S. strategy for 30+ years
  • The "Iron Curtain" was Winston Churchill's 1946 phrase for the line dividing the free West from the Soviet-controlled East
9
Containment in Greece and Turkey; the "Truman Doctrine."
CH. 28
  • Britain could no longer fund the anti-Communist side in Greece's civil war (1947)
  • The Truman Doctrine pledged aid — money, weapons, advisors — to Greece and Turkey
  • It was soon extended to any nation resisting a Communist takeover
10
The Marshall Plan; the reasons for the Berlin airlift.
CH. 28
  • The Marshall Plan (European Recovery Program, 1948–51, ~$13 billion) rebuilt Western Europe, blunted Communism, and boosted U.S. exports
  • Stalin's Berlin blockade (June 1948) cut land/water routes to West Berlin
  • The U.S., Britain, and France answered with the Berlin airlift (~2 million tons) until the Soviets relented (May 1949)
11
Containment in Asia: China, Korea, and the Korean War.
CH. 28
  • China: Mao Zedong's Communists won in 1949; Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists fled to Taiwan (a major setback)
  • Korea: Kim Il Sung invaded across the 38th parallel (June 25, 1950)
  • UN/U.S. forces under Douglas MacArthur landed at Inchon; China (Zhou Enlai) intervened; MacArthur was relieved (1951); armistice July 27, 1953
12
The Rosenbergs, HUAC (Hollywood hearings), McCarthyism, and the rise & fall of Sen. Joseph McCarthy.
CH. 28
  • Julius Rosenberg and Ethel Rosenberg were executed in 1953 for alleged atomic spying
  • HUAC investigated Hollywood, producing the blacklist (the Hollywood Ten)
  • Senator Joseph McCarthy built McCarthyism on unproven accusations; the televised Army-McCarthy Hearings (1954) led to his Senate censure
13
Postwar society: Eisenhower, suburbs, economy, baby boom, rock & roll, television; early civil rights.
CH. 28
  • Dwight D. Eisenhower (elected 1952) presided over prosperity and conformity
  • Suburbs boomed (William Levitt / Levittown; homeownership ~44% → 62%); the baby boom (1946–64); rock and roll (Alan Freed, Elvis Presley, Chuck Berry) + television
  • Early civil rights: Brown v. Board of Education (1954), the Little Rock Nine (1957), the Montgomery Bus Boycott (Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King, Jr., 1955–56)
14
The important factors of the 1960 presidential election.
CH. 29
  • John F. Kennedy vs. Richard M. Nixon; the first televised debate (Sept 26, 1960) favored the telegenic Kennedy
  • Kennedy's Roman Catholic faith worried Protestants; adding Protestant Texan Lyndon B. Johnson shored up the South
  • Kennedy won by the closest popular margin since 1888 and became the youngest elected president
15
Cold War events under JFK: Berlin Wall, Bay of Pigs, Cuban Missile Crisis, Peace Corps, Vietnam.
CH. 29
  • Bay of Pigs (1961): a failed CIA-backed invasion by Cuban exiles after Kennedy cancelled air support
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (Oct 1962): a naval "quarantine"; Nikita Khrushchev removed the missiles for a U.S. no-invasion pledge (plus a secret removal of U.S. missiles from Turkey)
  • Peace Corps (1961) = Cold War "soft power"; in Vietnam, Kennedy expanded aid and advisors (~16,000 troops by 1963) to Ngo Dinh Diem
  • ⚠️ Berlin Wall (built 1961) — flagged: the provided Ch 29 text doesn't describe its construction; verify from Prof. Walter's materials
16
LBJ's "Great Society" programs: their purposes and goals.
CH. 29
  • The Great Society / war on poverty aimed to end poverty and racial injustice
  • Education: Elementary and Secondary Education Act + Higher Education Act (1965)
  • Health: Medicare and Medicaid (Social Security Act, 1965)
  • Anti-poverty: Economic Opportunity Act (1964 — Job Corps, Head Start, VISTA); Immigration and Nationality Act (1965)
17
How U.S. involvement escalated in Vietnam under LBJ: Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, Tet Offensive, My Lai.
CH. 29 → 30
  • The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (Aug 1964) let Johnson wage war without a declaration; bombing + 200,000+ troops followed
  • The Tet Offensive (Jan 1968) turned public opinion against the war despite being militarily repelled
  • The My Lai massacre (March 16, 1968; Lt. William Calley) deepened disillusionment when exposed in 1969
18
Domestic society in the 1960s: civil rights (African Americans, women, American Indians, gays), counterculture.
CH. 29 → 30
  • African Americans: sit-ins, Freedom Rides, the March on Washington → Black Power (Stokely Carmichael; the Black Panthers)
  • Women: Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique, the birth control pill (1960), and NOW (1966)
  • American Indians: the American Indian Movement (1968) and Alcatraz; Gays/lesbians: the Stonewall uprising (1969); plus the hippie counterculture (Woodstock)
19
The 1968 election: LBJ pulls out, Democratic turmoil, Nixon's comeback, and the "Silent Majority."
CH. 30
  • After Tet and a strong showing by Eugene McCarthy, Johnson withdrew
  • The party split among Hubert Humphrey, McCarthy, and Robert Kennedy (assassinated in June); George Wallace ran as a segregationist third party
  • Amid the chaotic Chicago convention, Richard Nixon won using a southern strategy + law-and-order; his "Silent Majority" = northern, White, blue-collar voters
20
Nixon's "Vietnamization," his escalation into Cambodia, and his policy toward China.
CH. 30
  • Vietnamization shifted combat to South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing U.S. troops
  • Nixon secretly bombed and then invaded Cambodia (1970) to hit supply routes
  • Toward China, he reversed two decades of hostility, visiting Beijing (Feb 1972) to meet Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai — pressuring the USSR and isolating North Vietnam
21
Growth of the antiwar movement (Kent State, Pentagon Papers); how the war was "settled" and its aftermath.
CH. 30
  • The Cambodia invasion sparked protests — most tragically the Kent State shootings (May 4, 1970) and Jackson State
  • The Pentagon Papers (1971), leaked by Daniel Ellsberg, revealed government deception
  • Henry Kissinger and Le Duc Tho signed the Paris accord (Jan 1973); U.S. troops left, but ~100,000 North Vietnamese remained and Saigon fell (April 1975)
22
Watergate: the break-in, the cover-up, the investigation, and the fall of Nixon.
CH. 30
  • Operatives from CREEP (and the "plumbers") broke into DNC headquarters at the Watergate complex (June 17, 1972)
  • Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein (source "Deep Throat" = Mark Felt) plus Senate hearings exposed the cover-up; the Saturday Night Massacre and the Oval Office tapes sealed it
  • Facing impeachment, Nixon resigned (Aug 1974); Gerald Ford then pardoned him
23
Jimmy Carter's personality and policies; the Iran hostage crisis.
CH. 30
  • Jimmy Carter ran as a moral, "anti-Washington" outsider (born-again Christian, "I'll never lie")
  • Achievements: the Camp David Accords (1978, Menachem Begin & Anwar Sadat), the Department of Energy, deregulation — but stagflation persisted
  • The Iran hostage crisis: the embassy in Tehran was seized (Nov 4, 1979); 52 held 444 days, freed as Reagan took office (Jan 1981)
24
21st-century disputes: climate change, immigration, and same-sex marriage.
CH. 32.3
  • Climate change: the U.S. signed but never ratified the Kyoto Protocol (1998); President Bush objected on economic grounds; Al Gore's An Inconvenient Truth (2006) countered denial
  • Immigration: English-as-official-language pushes, the failed Comprehensive Immigration Reform Act (2006), and strict Arizona/Alabama enforcement laws
  • Same-sex marriage: the Defense of Marriage Act (1996) → Massachusetts first to allow it (2004) → California's Prop 8 (2008) → Supreme Court began overturning bans (2013)
Ch. 27 — Fighting the Good Fight in World War II
1941–1945
▼
Origins: Dictators & Appeasement
Benito Mussolini founded Fascism (Fasci Italiani di Combattimento, 1919) and became Italy's PM in 1922. Adolf Hitler's Nazi Party grew during mass unemployment; Paul von Hindenburg appointed him chancellor in Jan 1933, and the Enabling Act made him dictator. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of citizenship. Hitler remilitarized the Rhineland (1936) and annexed Austria (1938). At the Munich Conference (1938), Britain's Neville Chamberlain and France's Édouard Daladier handed Germany the Sudetenland — the Munich Pact, a policy of appeasement ("peace in our time"). WWII began with the Blitzkrieg on Poland, Sept 1, 1939; Britain and France declared war Sept 3.
FascismappeasementBlitzkrieg
Confused pacts — keep straight: Four Power Treaty (1921) → Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928, war = "international crime") → Anti-Comintern Pact (1936, Japan+Germany; +Italy 1937 = the Axis) → Pact of Steel (May 1939, Ger-Italy military) → Nazi-Soviet nonaggression pact (Aug 1939, split Poland).
From Neutrality to Engagement
Senator Gerald P. Nye led the noninterventionists; the Neutrality Acts (1935, 1936, 1937) banned arms sales, loans, and travel to belligerents. As war spread, Franklin D. Roosevelt aided Britain: Cash and Carry (Nov 1939), the Export Control Act (July 1940, embargo of Japan), and Lend-Lease (March 1941, ~$45 billion, which "ended nonintervention"). The Atlantic Charter (Aug 1941) laid out Anglo-American aims. Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor (Dec 7, 1941) — "a date which will live in infamy" — brought the U.S. in; Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. Dec 11.
Neutrality ActsLend-LeasePearl Harbor
U.S. neutrality steps, in order: Neutrality Acts (35/36/37) → Cash and Carry (1939) → Export Control Act (1940, embargo Japan) → Lend-Lease (1941). Don't blur these.
The Home Front
The first peacetime draft came in Sept 1940. A. Philip Randolph threatened a march → Executive Order 8802 created the Fair Employment Practices Committee. The Double V campaign (Pittsburgh Courier) sought victory abroad and over racism at home; the Tuskegee Airmen and Navajo code talkers served with distinction. Rosie the Riveter (Norman Rockwell) symbolized women workers. But Executive Order 9066 (Feb 19, 1942) forced ~120,000 people of Japanese ancestry into internment; the 442nd Regimental Combat Team became the most decorated unit of its size.
EO 8802EO 9066Double V
Three executive orders — don't mix up: EO 8802 (1941, fair employment / defense jobs) · EO 9066 (1942, Japanese internment) · EO 9835 (1947, loyalty program).
Victory in Europe & the Pacific
The Big Three — Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Joseph Stalin — met at Casablanca (1943, "unconditional surrender"), Tehran (1943), Yalta (1945), and Potsdam (1945). D-Day (June 6, 1944) opened the second front; the Battle of the Bulge (Dec 1944) was Germany's last offensive; Germany surrendered May 8, 1945. In the Pacific, Midway (June 1942) turned the tide, and Douglas MacArthur pursued island hopping through Iwo Jima and Okinawa. Harry S. Truman dropped "Little Boy" on Hiroshima (Aug 6) and "Fat Man" on Nagasaki (Aug 9); Emperor Hirohito surrendered.
D-DayMidwayManhattan Project
Turning points: Midway (Pacific) · Stalingrad (eastern front) · D-Day (western front) · Battle of the Bulge (Germany's last offensive). Bombs: Hiroshima Aug 6 / Nagasaki Aug 9; Kokura was the second bomb's intended primary but was cloud-covered.
Ch. 28 — Post-War Prosperity & Cold War Fears
1945–1960
▼
Peacetime, the GI Bill & the Fair Deal
Demobilization shrank the military from ~12 million to ~1.5 million by 1947. The GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act, 1944) gave veterans unemployment pay, tuition, and home loans — though African American, gay, and some Mexican American vets were shut out. Harry S. Truman's Fair Deal (1949) sought a higher minimum wage, Social Security expansion, and public housing; he desegregated the armed forces by executive order (1948). Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act (limiting unions) over his veto.
GI BillFair DealTaft-Hartley Act
Three "Deals": FDR's New Deal → Truman's Fair Deal → LBJ's Great Society.
Containment & the Cold War Abroad
George Kennan's "Long Telegram" (1946) launched containment; Winston Churchill named the "Iron Curtain" (1946). The National Security Act (1947) created the NSC and CIA. The Truman Doctrine (1947) aided Greece and Turkey; the Marshall Plan (1948–51, ~$13 billion) rebuilt Western Europe. Stalin's Berlin blockade met the Berlin airlift (1948–49) → NATO (1949). Then in 1949 the USSR got the atomic bomb and Mao Zedong's Communists won China (Chiang Kai-shek fled to Taiwan).
containmentMarshall PlanNATO
Cold War doctrines: containment (Kennan's idea) → Truman Doctrine (aid Greece & Turkey) → Marshall Plan (rebuild economies, $13B) → domino theory (Eisenhower framing).
Red Scare, Korea & McCarthy
Executive Order 9835 (1947) began the loyalty program; HUAC investigated Hollywood (the blacklist / Hollywood Ten); Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were executed (1953). In Korea, Kim Il Sung invaded across the 38th parallel (June 25, 1950); Douglas MacArthur's Inchon landing reversed the war until China (Zhou Enlai) intervened; MacArthur was relieved (1951); armistice July 27, 1953. Senator Joseph McCarthy's crusade collapsed after the televised Army-McCarthy Hearings (1954) and Senate censure.
HUACKorean WarMcCarthyism
The American Dream & Early Civil Rights
Dwight D. Eisenhower (elected 1952) embraced "massive retaliation" (MAD) and warned of the "military-industrial complex." Suburbs boomed (William Levitt / Levittown; homeownership ~44% → 62%); the baby boom (1946–64) and rock and roll (Alan Freed, Elvis Presley, Chuck Berry) reshaped culture; Sputnik (1957) spurred the National Defense Education Act (1958). Civil rights: Brown v. Board of Education (1954, Thurgood Marshall, Chief Justice Earl Warren), the Little Rock Nine (1957, Gov. Orval Faubus), Emmett Till (1955), and the Montgomery Bus Boycott (Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King, Jr., 1955–56).
Brown v. BoardLittle Rock NineMontgomery Bus Boycott
Cases: Plessy v. Ferguson (1896, "separate but equal") vs. Brown v. Board (1954, overturns it). Defense: Eisenhower = massive retaliation/MAD; Kennedy = flexible response.
Ch. 29 — Contesting Futures: America in the 1960s
1960–1968
▼
The Kennedy Promise
John F. Kennedy's "New Frontier" opened with the first televised debate (Sept 26, 1960) over Richard M. Nixon. His defense used flexible response (Robert McNamara), the Green Berets, and the Peace Corps (1961). The Bay of Pigs (1961) failed, but Kennedy prevailed in the Cuban Missile Crisis (Oct 1962) — a naval "quarantine" that made Nikita Khrushchev withdraw the missiles. In Vietnam he backed Ngo Dinh Diem (~16,000 advisors by 1963). He was assassinated in Dallas (Nov 22, 1963) — Lee Harvey Oswald, then Jack Ruby; the Warren Commission followed.
New FrontierCuban Missile CrisisPeace Corps
Wording trap: Kennedy called it a "quarantine," not a "blockade" (a blockade is an act of war). ⚠️ The Berlin Wall (1961) is in Objective 15 but not described in the provided Ch 29 text — verify from lecture.
Johnson's Great Society
Lyndon B. Johnson's Great Society / war on poverty delivered the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), Medicare and Medicaid (Social Security Act, 1965), the Economic Opportunity Act (1964 — Job Corps, Head Start, VISTA), and the Immigration and Nationality Act (1965). Civil rights: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), the 24th Amendment (poll tax), and — after Selma's "Bloody Sunday" (Gov. George Wallace) — the Voting Rights Act of 1965. But the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) and the Tet Offensive (1968) drove escalation and public disillusionment.
Great SocietyVoting Rights ActGulf of Tonkin
Civil-rights laws: CRA 1960 (voting referees) · CRA 1964 (public accommodations + jobs, Title VII) · Voting Rights Act 1965 (ends literacy tests) · 24th Amendment 1964 (poll tax) · CRA 1968 (housing). Tonkin: incident (Aug 1964) vs. Resolution (Aug 7, war without a declaration).
The Movement & Challenging the Status Quo
Grassroots action drove change: the Greensboro sit-in (1960) → SNCC (Ella Baker); Freedom Rides (1961, John Lewis); the March on Washington ("I Have a Dream," 1963); the "Letter from Birmingham Jail"; Freedom Summer (1964). After Martin Luther King, Jr.'s assassination (April 1968), many turned to Black Power — Stokely Carmichael, Malcolm X, and the Black Panthers (Bobby Seale, Huey Newton). The Chicano movement (Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta), the New Left (SDS / Port Huron Statement), and the women's movement (Betty Friedan, the pill, NOW) all rose.
SNCCBlack PowerNOW
Ch. 30 — Political Storms at Home & Abroad
1968–1980
▼
Identity Politics
The counterculture (hippies, communes, Woodstock 1969) rejected mainstream norms. The American Indian Movement (1968; Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt) occupied Alcatraz (1969–71) and Wounded Knee (1973). The Stonewall uprising (June 28, 1969) launched gay liberation; Harvey Milk won office in 1977. Feminists won Roe v. Wade (1973) and Title IX (Education Amendments, 1972, Patsy Mink); Shirley Chisholm ran for president (1972). The ERA passed Congress (1972) but was never ratified.
AIMStonewallRoe v. Wade
Nixon: Politics, Economy & Diplomacy
Richard Nixon won 1968 (over Hubert Humphrey and George Wallace) with a southern strategy and appeals to the "Silent Majority"; Spiro Agnew was his VP. The economy brought stagflation; the 1973 OAPEC oil embargo (after the Yom Kippur War) sent gas prices soaring. Diplomacy was his strength: the Nixon Doctrine (1969), the historic China visit (Feb 1972, Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai), and détente + SALT with Leonid Brezhnev (Moscow, 1972).
Silent MajoritydétenteSALT
Arms/détente by president: SALT I (Nixon, 1972) · Helsinki Accords (Ford, 1975) · SALT II (Carter). Doctrines: Nixon Doctrine (1969, allies self-defend) vs. Carter Doctrine (1980, force in the Middle East).
Vietnam's Downward Spiral
The My Lai massacre (March 16, 1968; Lt. William Calley), exposed in 1969, horrified the public. Nixon's Vietnamization withdrew troops while he bombed and then invaded Cambodia (1970) → the Kent State (May 4) and Jackson State (May 15) shootings. The Pentagon Papers (1971, Daniel Ellsberg) revealed deception. Henry Kissinger and Le Duc Tho signed the Paris accord (Jan 1973); Saigon fell April 30, 1975.
VietnamizationKent StatePentagon Papers
Two campus shootings: Kent State (May 4, 1970, Ohio, 4 killed) and Jackson State (May 15, 1970, Mississippi, 2 killed) — both after the Cambodia announcement.
Watergate, Ford & Carter
The Watergate break-in (June 17, 1972) at DNC headquarters, exposed by Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein (source "Deep Throat" = Mark Felt), brought down Nixon (resigned Aug 1974). Gerald Ford pardoned him. Jimmy Carter (elected 1976) brokered the Camp David Accords (1978, Menachem Begin & Anwar Sadat), but stagflation and the Iran hostage crisis (Nov 4, 1979; 52 held 444 days) doomed his presidency.
WatergateDeep ThroatIran hostage crisis
Carter's Middle East: Camp David Accords (1978, framework) vs. the Egypt-Israel peace treaty (1979) — different things.
Ch. 32.3 — New Century, Old Disputes
21st-Century Issues (this section only)
▼
Immigration
As the White majority approached minority status, some pushed to make English the official language (Arizona, 2006). The Comprehensive Immigration Reform Act (2006) — border security + guest workers + a path to legal status — failed. During the Great Recession, Arizona and Alabama passed strict enforcement laws (partly struck down); in 2013 a surge of ~52,000 Central American children reached the border.
official-language lawsComprehensive Immigration Reform Act (2006)
Same-Sex Marriage
The Defense of Marriage Act (1996) barred federal recognition. After Vermont's civil unions, Massachusetts became the first state to allow same-sex marriage (2004). Between 1998–2012, 30 states banned it (including California's Proposition 8, 2008), but courts — and the Supreme Court (2013) — began overturning the bans.
DOMA (1996)Massachusetts 2004Proposition 8
Keep straight: DOMA (1996, bars federal recognition) vs. Massachusetts (2004, first state to allow marriage) vs. Prop 8 (2008, California state ban).
Climate Change
The U.S. (~36% of greenhouse gases) signed the Kyoto Protocol (1998) but never ratified it; President Bush objected on economic grounds, and the administration was accused of suppressing climate science. Al Gore's documentary An Inconvenient Truth (2006) and his 2007 Nobel Peace Prize (shared with the IPCC) pushed back.
Kyoto Protocol (1998)An Inconvenient Truth
