1
What was the Progressive Era? How did it impact American life, and what were its features? Who were the muckrakers?
CH. 21 · Sec 21.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 1
The Progressive Era (approximately 1890–1920) was a broad reform movement responding to Gilded Age ills — urban poverty, corruption, monopolies, dangerous working conditions, and racial violence. Progressives united around three core goals: expanding democracy, improving efficiency in government, and promoting social justice. Reform began at the grassroots; federal involvement came later under Theodore Roosevelt.
Key People — The Muckrakers
Ida Tarbell
Exposed Standard Oil's monopolistic practices in a series of articles in McClure's magazine.
Upton Sinclair
Wrote The Jungle (1905) exposing horrific conditions in Chicago's meatpacking industry. Triggered the Meat Inspection Act (1906).
Lincoln Steffens
Investigated and exposed corruption in city governments across the United States.
Jacob Riis
How the Other Half Lives (1890) documented NYC slum tenement conditions with photographs.
Key Terms
muckrakersyellow journalismgrassroots Progressivismsocial justice
Remember
  • Theodore Roosevelt coined "muckraker" — from John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress. He meant it as criticism; journalists wore it as a badge.
  • Unlike sensationalist "yellow journalists," muckrakers had explicit reform goals — they wanted public outrage to lead to legislation.
2
Discuss Progressive movements at the local & state levels: direct primary, initiative, referendum, and recall. Describe Progressivism in social justice, labor, and women's rights.
CH. 21 · Sec 21.2
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 2
Progressives introduced four tools of direct democracy to reduce the power of party bosses and give citizens more control. They also tackled labor, child labor, alcohol, and women's rights.
  • Direct Primary — Voters (not convention delegates) select party candidates. South Carolina first (1896).
  • Initiative — Citizens petition to place a new law on the ballot. South Dakota first (1898).
  • Referendum — Voters affirm or reject an existing law.
  • Recall — Citizens remove a public official via petition and vote. Oregon first (1910).
  • 17th Amendment (1913) — Direct election of U.S. senators (previously chosen by state legislatures).
  • Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire (March 25, 1911) — 146 workers killed, exits locked. Galvanized workplace safety laws.
  • Florence Kelley (National Consumers League) — pushed for 8-hour workday and safer factory conditions.
  • William "Big Bill" Haywood founded the IWW (Industrial Workers of the World / Wobblies) in 1905.
  • Eugene Victor Debs led the Socialist Party of America; ran for president five times.
  • 18th Amendment (1919) — Prohibition; driven by WCTU and Anti-Saloon League.
Key Terms
direct primaryinitiativereferendumrecall17th AmendmentTriangle Shirtwaist Fire18th Amendment
3
What was the Progressive Era? How did it impact American life, especially politics? Identify key reforms achieved.
CH. 21 · Sec 21.2–21.3
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 3
The Progressive Era fundamentally reshaped American politics by expanding participation, changing how candidates were selected, and expanding what government was responsible for. Key reforms shifted power from party machines to citizens and expert administrators.
  • City commission system — Galveston, Texas (1901); each commissioner ran one city function; reduced boss corruption.
  • City manager system — Staunton, Virginia (1908); elected council hires a professional manager.
  • Robert Marion "Fighting Bob" La Follette — Progressive governor and senator from Wisconsin; model of state-level reform against special interests.
  • 16th Amendment (1913) — Authorized a federal income tax; fundamental shift in how the federal government was funded.
  • Federal Reserve Act (1913) — Created the central banking system under Woodrow Wilson; stabilized the money supply.
Key Terms
16th AmendmentFederal Reserve Act 1913commission systemcity manager system
4
Explain how Theodore Roosevelt became President, even though he was not considered "conservative" by Republican Party leaders.
CH. 21 · Sec 21.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 4
Theodore Roosevelt became president on September 14, 1901 when President William McKinley was assassinated at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York. Roosevelt was only 42 — the youngest person ever to become president. Republican conservative bosses, including Senator Mark Hanna, had intentionally placed Roosevelt on the VP ticket to neutralize his Progressive influence — they considered the VP spot a political dead end. Hanna reportedly said: "Now look! That damned cowboy is now president!" The plan backfired spectacularly.
Key People
Theodore Roosevelt
Youngest president in history at 42. Former NY Governor, former Assistant Secretary of the Navy, Rough Riders leader in Cuba. Republicans considered him too Progressive and too popular to leave in a powerful office.
William McKinley
25th President; shot by anarchist Leon Czolgosz at the Pan-American Exposition; died September 14, 1901.
Key Terms
Pan-American Exposition 1901accidental president
5
Evaluate Theodore Roosevelt as president: his handling of the trusts (Northern Securities, Standard Oil); the "Square Deal" regarding labor disputes (Anthracite Coal Strike); safer food & drugs (Meat Inspection Act, Pure Food & Drug Act); and land conservation.
CH. 21 · Sec 21.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 5
Roosevelt's Square Deal transformed the presidency into an active guardian of the public good — a dramatic break from Gilded Age hands-off government. His agenda covered four major areas.
  • Trust-busting: Filed suit against Northern Securities Trust (1902) — railroad monopoly controlled by John Pierpont Morgan and John D. Rockefeller. Won 5-4 at Supreme Court (1904). Also sued Standard Oil and the American Tobacco Company. Over 44 antitrust suits total. Distinguished "good trusts" from "bad trusts."
  • Labor / Square Deal: Anthracite Coal Strike (1902) — invited both the United Mine Workers and mine owners to the White House to negotiate. First president to side with workers rather than deploy troops against them.
  • Food Safety: After reading The Jungle by Upton Sinclair: Meat Inspection Act (1906) established federal meat inspection; Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) required accurate labeling and banned adulterated goods.
  • Conservation: Appointed Gifford Pinchot as first chief of the U.S. Forestry Service. Created 18 national monuments, 5 national parks, and 150+ national forests — approximately 230 million acres total.
  • Railroad regulation: Elkins Act (1903) and Hepburn Act (1906) strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission's authority over railroad rates.
Key Terms
Square DealNorthern Securities TrustAnthracite Coal Strike 1902Meat Inspection Act 1906Pure Food and Drug Act 1906Gifford Pinchot
6
Name the man Theodore Roosevelt personally selected as his successor. Describe how Taft's personality and leadership style differed from Roosevelt's, and how Taft's presidency went wrong.
CH. 21 · Sec 21.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 6
Roosevelt hand-picked William Howard Taft — his Secretary of War and former Governor-General of the Philippines — as his successor. Taft won the 1908 election easily, defeating three-time Democratic nominee William Jennings Bryan. Despite strong credentials, Taft's presidency disappointed Progressives and Roosevelt himself.
  • Personality: Roosevelt was charismatic and combative, loved using the presidency as a "bully pulpit." Taft was cautious, judicial-minded, and uncomfortable with political combat.
  • What Taft did right: Initiated twice as many antitrust suits as Roosevelt. Signed the Mann-Elkins Act (1910) extending ICC authority to telephones and telegraphs.
  • Payne-Aldrich Act (1909): Taft endorsed a Senate bill that raised tariffs on over 800 products — enraging Progressives who wanted tariff reductions.
  • Pinchot-Ballinger Affair: Taft's Secretary of the Interior, Richard Ballinger, approved selling federal land to a company he had previously represented. Gifford Pinchot publicly condemned it. Taft fired Pinchot — which infuriated Roosevelt and widened the rift.
Key Terms
William Howard TaftPayne-Aldrich Act 1909Mann-Elkins Act 1910Ballinger-Pinchot Affair

CH. 21 · Sec 21.4

The Four Candidates — 1912
Theodore Roosevelt — Progressive (Bull Moose) Party
New Nationalism: powerful federal government regulates big business for the public good. Bolted the GOP after conservatives denied him the Republican nomination. Survived assassination attempt in Milwaukee; declared "It takes more than a bullet to kill a bull moose!" Finished second.

Describe Wilson's career background. Identify his Progressive "New Freedom" agenda and key reforms (Federal Reserve, Underwood Tariff, Clayton Act). How did WWI cause his Progressive agenda to be sidelined?
CH. 21 · Sec 21.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 21 Objective 8
Woodrow Wilson was a former history professor and president of Princeton University, then Progressive governor of New Jersey. His "New Freedom" promised a smaller, less interventionist federal government that would restore market competition by busting trusts — contrasting Roosevelt's big-government regulation approach.
  • Phase 1 (1913–1914): Underwood Tariff Act — reduced tariffs; established graduated federal income tax (16th Amendment). Federal Reserve Act (1913) — created the Federal Reserve central banking system.
  • Phase 2 (1916): Clayton Antitrust Act — strengthened antitrust law; protected labor unions. Federal child labor laws (Keating-Owen Act, 1916). 8-hour workday for railroad workers (Adamson Act, 1916).
  • WWI sidelined the agenda: After the U.S. entered WWI in April 1917, national security consumed Wilson's presidency. Progressive reform goals were pushed aside in favor of wartime mobilization.
Key Terms
New FreedomUnderwood Tariff ActFederal Reserve Act 1913Clayton Antitrust ActKeating-Owen Act

Explain America's expansionist impulse of the 1890s. Discuss Turner's Frontier Thesis and Mahan's arguments for a strong navy, overseas bases, and a Central American canal.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 9
Two thinkers provided the intellectual framework for American overseas expansion in the 1890s, as the continental frontier closed and American industry needed new markets.
Key Thinkers
Frederick Jackson Turner
Frontier Thesis (1893): The encounter between European traditions and the western wilderness shaped American democracy and character — but the frontier was now closed. This raised the question: where does America expand next? Politicians used Turner's argument to justify overseas expansion as a new frontier.
Alfred Thayer Mahan
The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890): America needed (1) a large modern steel navy, (2) overseas naval bases and coaling stations, and (3) a canal across Central America. Congress passed the Naval Act of 1890 immediately after.
William Henry Seward
Secretary of State (1861–1869). Purchased Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million (1867) — mocked as "Seward's Folly." Also annexed the Midway Islands. Laid early groundwork for overseas expansion.
Key Terms
Frontier Thesis (Turner)Sea Power / MahanSeward's FollyNaval Act 1890
10
Describe U.S. involvement in the Pacific and acquisition of Hawaii: the sugar industry, Queen Liliuokalani, the American-backed overthrow, and annexation in 1898.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 10
Hawaii's annexation illustrates how American economic interests drove territorial expansion — not military conquest alone, but corporate influence backed by U.S. diplomatic and military muscle.
  • By 1890, American sugar planters dominated Hawaii's economy; Hawaii exported nearly all its sugar to the U.S. tariff-free.
  • Queen Liliuokalani (1891–1893) tried to reassert Hawaiian sovereignty against American economic dominance. American businessmen, working with U.S. Minister to Hawaii John Stevens, staged an armed revolt against her government.
  • President Grover Cleveland initially refused to annex Hawaii, finding the overthrow illegal. President William McKinley and Congress formally annexed Hawaii in 1898 during the Spanish-American War for its strategic Pacific location.
  • Hawaii became a U.S. territory in 1900; residents gained U.S. citizenship.
Key Terms
Queen LiliuokalaniHawaii annexation 1898
11
Describe U.S. involvement in Cuba, the Spanish-American War, the Treaty of Paris (1898), and land acquired. Discuss the Philippine-American War, Emilio Aguinaldo, and the Taft Commission.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.2
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 11
  • Causes: Cuba's independence struggle; sensationalist yellow journalism by William Randolph Hearst and Joseph Pulitzer; mysterious explosion of the USS Maine in Havana Harbor (February 15, 1898) — "Remember the Maine!"
  • Teller Amendment: U.S. pledged before the war not to annex Cuba after defeating Spain.
  • The War: Lasted ~4 months. Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders charged San Juan Hill, Cuba. U.S. Navy destroyed the Spanish fleet in both the Caribbean and Manila Bay.
  • Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898): Spain recognized Cuba's independence; transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million.
  • Anti-Imperialist League (1898): Jane Addams, Andrew Carnegie, Mark Twain, Samuel Gompers. Opposed the treaty; Senate barely ratified it.
  • Platt Amendment: Gave U.S. right to intervene in Cuba; secured Guantanamo Bay. Cuba was "independent" only on paper.
  • Philippine-American War (1899–1902): Emilio Aguinaldo led Filipino resistance. 3 years; ~4,000 Americans killed, ~20,000 Filipino combatants, estimated 250,000 civilian deaths.
  • Taft Commission: William Howard Taft as civil governor; built infrastructure, schools, hospitals; offered political appointments to rebel leaders. Emilio Aguinaldo captured and forced to swear allegiance. Philippines under U.S. control until 1946.
Key Terms
USS MaineTeller AmendmentRough RidersTreaty of Paris 1898Anti-Imperialist LeaguePlatt AmendmentEmilio AguinaldoTaft Commission

Discuss U.S. interest in Asia and the "Open Door" policy toward China. Explain Secretary Hay's Open Door notes and how they favored American trade. Describe the Boxer Rebellion and U.S. response.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.3
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 12
European powers had carved China into spheres of influence — exclusive trade zones. The U.S. had no sphere and wanted all of China open to American goods.
Key Figure
Secretary of State John Hay
Issued the Open Door notes (1899) — circular letters demanding equal trade access to all Chinese ports for all nations. No special tariffs, no transportation monopolies. Overwhelmingly benefited the U.S. because American goods were cheaper and higher quality, flooding the Chinese market.
  • Other nations gave lukewarm replies, but Secretary Hay declared it official U.S. policy regardless.
  • Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901): Chinese nationalists (Righteous and Harmonious Fists) rose up to expel all Western influence. The U.S., Britain, and Germany sent ~2,000 troops to suppress it. Reinforced U.S. commitment to China's territorial integrity and the Open Door.
Key Terms
Open Door notes (Hay, 1899)sphere of influenceBoxer Rebellion 1899–1901
13
Discuss foreign policy under Theodore Roosevelt: Latin America ("Big Stick" diplomacy and the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine); the Panama Canal; and other diplomatic initiatives including the Russo-Japanese War.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 13
Roosevelt's foreign policy rested on an African proverb: "Speak softly, and carry a big stick, and you will go far." Rather than actually using military force, he threatened it credibly. Three major applications follow.
  • Panama Canal: Colombia rejected Roosevelt's treaty offer ($10M + $250K/year). Roosevelt signaled support for a Panamanian revolution (1903); sent U.S. battleships to block Colombian troops; recognized the Republic of Panama within a week. Canal opened August 1914. Roosevelt became the first sitting U.S. president to travel internationally (visited the construction site).
  • Roosevelt Corollary (1904): Roosevelt's address to Congress extended the Monroe Doctrine. The U.S. would act as an "international police power" in Latin America, intervening to correct "chronic wrongdoing." Unlike the Monroe Doctrine (which told Europe to stay out), the Roosevelt Corollary asserted the U.S. right to go in. Used to establish protectorates over Cuba, Panama, and the Dominican Republic.
  • Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905): Roosevelt mediated peace at Portsmouth, New Hampshire. Won the Nobel Peace Prize (1906) — but Japan felt cheated out of fair reparations, beginning long-term tensions with the U.S.
Key Terms
Big Stick diplomacyPanama Canal 1914Roosevelt Corollary 1904Monroe DoctrineNobel Peace Prize
14
Describe Taft's "Dollar Diplomacy" — substituting dollars for bullets. Explain how it worked in Central America and Asia, and what problems it created long-term.
CH. 22 · Sec 22.5
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22 Objective 14
When William Howard Taft became president in 1909, he adapted Roosevelt's foreign policy to rely on economic power rather than military threats. He announced his goal to "substitute dollars for bullets."
  • Central America: Taft paid off Central American debts owed to European powers with U.S. loans — making those countries indebted to the U.S. instead. When Nicaragua refused U.S. loans, Taft sent warships and marines to pressure the government.
  • Lodge Corollary: When Mexico considered allowing a Japanese corporation to obtain strategic land, Taft urged Congress to pass the Lodge Corollary — stating no foreign corporation (other than American ones) could acquire strategic lands in the Western Hemisphere.
  • Asia: Tried to bolster China's railroad development against Japanese expansion. Efforts backfired — alienated Japan and heightened U.S.-Japan tensions contributing to World War II.
  • Long-term damage: Created economic instability and nationalist resentment in Central America; heightened tensions with Japan.
Key Terms
Dollar DiplomacyLodge Corollary"substitute dollars for bullets"

Discuss the political situation in Mexico and how Wilson tried to handle it. Explain Wilson's overall foreign policy philosophy vs. his actual interventionist actions.
CH. 22–23 · Sec 22.5 / 23.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 22–23 Objective 15
Woodrow Wilson promised a less expansionist, more morally grounded foreign policy than Roosevelt or Taft. Yet Wilson found it nearly impossible to avoid intervention.
  • Philosophy vs. reality: Wilson believed the U.S. should only intervene abroad when there was a clear moral imperative. In practice, he sent troops into Haiti, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and Nicaragua — often protecting American business interests, just as his predecessors had.
  • Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan — noted anti-imperialist; negotiated "cooling off treaties"; offered Colombia a $25 million apology for Roosevelt's Panama actions.
  • Mexico: Wilson refused to recognize the government of Victoriano Huerta (who seized power by assassination). U.S. forces briefly occupied Veracruz (1914). Later, revolutionary leader Pancho Villa raided Columbus, New Mexico (1916), killing American civilians. Wilson sent General John "Black Jack" Pershing on a failed punitive expedition into Mexico to capture Villa. Pershing never caught him and withdrew in 1917 as WWI loomed.

16
Describe the conditions and events (~1900–1914) that led to WWI in Europe. Name the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire) and Allied Powers (Britain, France, Russia, Italy) at the outset of war.
CH. 23 · Sec 23.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 16
WWI began on July 28, 1914, triggered by a single assassination but rooted in decades of European nationalism, imperial rivalry, an arms race, and interlocking alliances that turned a regional conflict into a world war.
  • Spark: Archduke Franz Ferdinand — heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne — was assassinated by Gavrilo Princip, a Serbian nationalist, in Sarajevo, Bosnia on June 28, 1914.
  • Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire (and later Bulgaria).
  • Allied Powers (Entente): Britain, France, Russia; later joined by Italy (1915), Japan, and the United States (1917).
  • Underlying causes: Intense nationalism (especially in the Balkans); imperial rivalries; massive military buildup (arms race); a system of secret alliances that meant a local conflict could drag in all major powers.
  • The Western Front quickly became brutal trench warfare stalemate — both sides dug in across hundreds of miles of France and Belgium; millions died for small territorial gains.
Key Terms
Franz Ferdinand assassinationCentral PowersAllied Powers / Ententetrench warfareWestern Front
17
Why did the U.S. declare neutrality when WWI broke out, and why was this policy difficult to maintain? Explain Wilson's dilemma between free trade and neutrality.
CH. 23 · Sec 23.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 17
President Woodrow Wilson declared neutrality in August 1914. His 1916 reelection slogan was "He kept us out of war." But neutrality proved nearly impossible to sustain.
  • Why neutrality initially made sense: Millions of Americans were recent immigrants with strong ethnic ties to both sides. Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan was a committed anti-imperialist. American businesses profitably traded with all sides.
  • The dilemma: U.S. had profitable free trade with Allied nations (especially Britain and France). Germany's submarine warfare targeted all shipping to Britain, including American ships. Wilson insisted on the right to trade freely — which put American ships in German crosshairs.
  • Why neutrality was hard: American banks and businesses had lent enormous sums to Britain and France; if the Allies lost, those loans would default. The U.S. economy was effectively entangled with Allied success.
Key Terms
neutralityU-boats / submarinesfree trade"He kept us out of war"
18
Discuss the fighting in WWI in the years prior to U.S. involvement: trench warfare, stalemate on the Western Front, the scale of European casualties.
CH. 23 · Sec 23.1
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 18
Before the U.S. entered the war in April 1917, Europe had been fighting for nearly three years in one of the bloodiest conflicts in human history. Understanding the scale of slaughter helps explain why Americans were reluctant to join.
  • Trench warfare: Both sides dug massive trench systems across hundreds of miles of France and Belgium. Soldiers lived in the trenches for months, exposed to mud, disease, rats, and constant shelling. "Going over the top" meant near-certain death from machine guns and artillery.
  • Scale of casualties (pre-U.S. entry): By 1917, the Allies had suffered ~5 million military deaths; Germany and Austria-Hungary roughly 3 million more. The Battle of the Somme (1916) alone killed over 1 million men.
  • New weapons of mass killing: Machine guns, artillery, poison gas (first used by Germany in 1915), tanks, and aircraft made this war far deadlier than any before it.
  • Stalemate: Despite enormous casualties, neither side gained significant ground on the Western Front for three years.
Key Terms
trench warfareWestern Front stalemateBattle of the Somme 1916poison gas
19
Explain the events that forced the U.S. into the war: German U-boat warfare; the sinking of the Lusitania (1915); the Zimmermann Telegram (1917); and the U.S. declaration of war in April 1917.
CH. 23 · Sec 23.1–23.2
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 19
The U.S. did not enter WWI because of a single event but a series of escalating German provocations that made neutrality politically and economically untenable.
  • German U-boats: Germany declared a war zone around Britain; submarines attacked ships without warning, violating international law (ships were supposed to warn crews before sinking them).
  • Sinking of the RMS Lusitania (May 7, 1915): British ocean liner torpedoed off the coast of Ireland. 1,198 killed including 128 Americans. Germany agreed to the Sussex Pledge (1916) — promising to limit submarine warfare.
  • Germany resumes unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1917): Desperate to break the Allied naval blockade, Germany announced it would sink all ships — including American — without warning. Directly violated the Sussex Pledge.
  • Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917): German Foreign Minister Arthur Zimmermann secretly proposed an alliance with Mexico: if the U.S. entered the war, Mexico should join Germany and could reclaim Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. British intelligence intercepted and decoded the telegram; Wilson released it to the public. Outrage was immediate.
  • Declaration of War (April 6, 1917): Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war. Congress voted yes, though 56 members voted against it.
Key Terms
Lusitania May 7, 1915Sussex Pledge 1916Zimmermann Telegram 1917Arthur ZimmermannDeclaration of War April 6, 1917

Describe the "home front" during WWI: Creel Committee propaganda, the Espionage Act (1917), Sedition Act (1918), anti-German sentiment, and Schenck v. United States ("clear and present danger").
CH. 23 · Sec 23.2–23.3
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 20
The federal government mobilized public opinion through propaganda while suppressing dissent through sweeping laws that restricted free speech more severely than any legislation since 1798.
Key Figures
George Creel
Directed the Committee on Public Information (CPI). Produced anti-German posters, pamphlets, films. Organized "Four-Minute Men" who gave short pro-war speeches at movie theaters.
Eugene Victor Debs
Socialist Party leader; sentenced to 10 years in prison under the Espionage Act for encouraging young men to resist the draft.
Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes
Wrote the unanimous Supreme Court opinion in Schenck v. United States (1919); created the "clear and present danger" standard for limiting First Amendment speech.
  • Espionage Act (1917): Prohibited aiding the enemy; up to 20-year sentences for opposing the war or interfering with military recruitment. Over 2,000 people charged.
  • Sedition Act (1918): Extended restrictions to any criticism of the government, Constitution, military, or American flag.
  • Schenck v. United States (1919): Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote: speech presenting a "clear and present danger" is not protected — compared anti-draft pamphlets to falsely shouting "fire!" in a crowded theater.
  • Anti-German sentiment: Sauerkraut renamed "liberty cabbage"; German street names changed; anti-German hysteria actually accelerated Prohibition (German-owned breweries targeted).
Key Terms
Creel Committee / CPIEspionage Act 1917Sedition Act 1918Schenck v. United States"clear and present danger"Oliver Wendell Holmes

Describe U.S. military activities during WWI: the AEF under Pershing, the Harlem Hellfighters, the Meuse-Argonne Offensive, and the armistice of November 11, 1918. What were American and Allied casualties?
CH. 23 · Sec 23.4
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 21
American military involvement in WWI was late but decisive. Fresh American soldiers — the "doughboys" — helped break the German stalemate after European armies were exhausted.
Key Figures
General John "Black Jack" Pershing
Commanded the American Expeditionary Forces (AEF). Insisted U.S. troops fight as an independent army, not be split up to reinforce Allied units. Previously led the failed punitive expedition against Pancho Villa in Mexico.
369th Infantry — "Harlem Hellfighters"
All-Black regiment that served on the French frontline for 6 months — longer than any other American unit. Fought under French command. 171 men received the French Legion of Merit for meritorious service in combat.
  • Meuse-Argonne Offensive (September–November 1918): Over 1 million U.S. troops attacked German lines. 47 days of intense fighting broke the German military's ability to continue the war.
  • Armistice — November 11, 1918: Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated November 9. Armistice took effect at 11 AM on the 11th hour of the 11th day of the 11th month.
  • American casualties: 117,000 killed; 206,000 wounded. Total Allied military deaths: ~5.7 million. Central Powers: ~4 million. War cost the U.S. over $32 billion.

22
Discuss Wilson's "14 Points" and the Treaty of Versailles: to what degree did the treaty include the 14 Points? How was the treaty punitive toward Germany (war guilt clause, reparations)? Why did the U.S. Senate not ratify it (Irreconcilables vs. Reservationists)? Discuss demobilization aftermath: Red Scare, Red Summer, flu pandemic, and Harding's election.
CH. 23 · Sec 23.4–23.5
▾
Study Breakdown · Ch. 23 Objective 22
This objective covers the entire postwar sequence — Wilson's vision, its betrayal at Versailles, the Senate fight, and the traumatic domestic aftermath that set the stage for the 1920s.
  • Fourteen Points (January 8, 1918): Wilson's postwar vision: free trade, freedom of the seas, no secret treaties, self-determination of peoples, reduction of armaments, and a League of Nations. Announced five months before U.S. troops fired their first shot.
  • Paris Peace Conference (Dec 1918–June 1919): Wilson attended personally — unprecedented. Britain's David Lloyd George, France's Georges Clemenceau, and Italy's Vittorio Orlando wanted Germany punished, not "peace without victory."
  • Treaty of Versailles (June 1919): Far harsher than Wilson's 14 Points. Germany forced to accept the "war guilt clause" (Article 231, sole responsibility for WWI); over $33 billion in reparations; surrender of territories; military disarmament. League of Nations included but the U.S. never joined.
  • Senate battle: Irreconcilables (led by Senator William Borah) opposed the treaty entirely. Reservationists (led by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge) would support it with amendments removing Article X. Wilson refused to compromise. Went on cross-country tour; suffered a debilitating stroke (September 25, 1919). His wife Edith Wilson effectively ran the presidency for ~six months. Senate voted down the treaty twice. U.S. technically at war with Germany until Knox-Porter Resolution (July 1921).
  • Flu Pandemic (1918–1919): ~675,000 Americans died; worldwide ~500 million infected, ~50 million deaths.
  • Red Scare (1919–1920): Fear of Communist revolution. Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer and his assistant J. Edgar Hoover organized raids; ~4,000 arrested; ~250 deported on the "Soviet Ark."
  • Red Summer (1919): 25+ race riots; 250+ killed. Chicago Race Riot: triggered when a White mob stoned a Black teenager at Lake Michigan; police didn't arrest the perpetrator; week-long riot, 38 dead. Tulsa Massacre (1921): White mob destroyed Greenwood ("Black Wall Street"); 50–300 Black deaths; suppressed from history for decades.
  • Election of 1920: Senator Warren Gamaliel Harding won on "return to normalcy" — 404 electoral votes, 60% popular vote. Americans exhausted by war, flu, Red Scare, racial violence, and Progressive government interventions.

Chapter 21 — The Progressive Movement (1890–1920)
Muckrakers · Democracy · Women & African Americans · TR, Taft, Wilson
▾
What Was Progressivism?
The Progressive Era was a response to the social ills of the Gilded Age — urban sprawl, corruption, dangerous working conditions, monopolies, and racial violence. Progressives shared three core goals: expanding democracy, improving efficiency in government and business, and promoting social justice. Most Progressives were educated, middle-class Americans, but the movement included women, African Americans, urban and rural dwellers.

Key terms: muckrakers grassroots Progressivism social justice
The Muckrakers
Investigative journalists who exposed social problems and urged public action — unlike "yellow journalists" who merely sensationalized. Theodore Roosevelt coined the nickname "muckraker" (from a character in Pilgrim's Progress). Key figures:
Ida Tarbell
Exposed Standard Oil's monopolistic practices; articles in McClure's
Upton Sinclair
The Jungle (1905) — exposed meatpacking industry; led to Meat Inspection Act
Lincoln Steffens
Exposed corruption in city politics
Jacob Riis
How the Other Half Lives (1890) — photographed NYC slum conditions
Grassroots Democracy Reforms
Progressives pushed three key tools of direct democracy:
• Direct primary — voters (not party bosses) select candidates; replaced convention delegate system. South Carolina first (1896).
• Initiative — citizens petition to place legislation on the ballot. South Dakota first (1898).
• Referendum — voters affirm or reject existing laws.
• Recall — citizens remove an official via petition + vote. Oregon first (1910).
• 17th Amendment (1913) — direct election of U.S. Senators (previously chosen by state legislatures).

Municipal reform: Galveston, TX commission system (1901) after the 1900 hurricane; city manager system (Staunton, VA, 1908).

Key terms: direct primary initiative referendum recall 17th Amendment
Social Justice: Labor, Children & Prohibition
• Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire (March 25, 1911) — 146 garment workers (mostly young immigrant women) died; management had locked exits. Galvanized workplace safety laws.
• National Child Labor Committee — pushed for Keating-Owen Act (1916) banning interstate sale of goods made with child labor; eventually the Fair Labor Standards Act (1938) made it permanent.
• Florence Kelley (National Consumers League) — pushed for 8-hour workday and safer factories.
• WCTU & Anti-Saloon League — prohibition movement; led to 18th Amendment (ratified 1919), prohibiting manufacture, sale, and transportation of alcohol.
• IWW (Wobblies) — "Big Bill" Haywood's radical industrial union; favored general strike and direct action. Founded 1905.
• SPA (Socialist Party of America) — Eugene Debs; ran for president 5 times; over 70 Socialist mayors elected 1901–1918.

Key terms: Triangle Shirtwaist Fire 1911 18th Amendment IWW / Wobblies Eugene Debs
Women's Suffrage Movement
• Roots: Seneca Falls Convention (1848) — Declaration of Sentiments, first demand for women's vote.
• NAWSA (National American Women's Suffrage Association, 1890) — led by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony; later by Carrie Chapman Catt (from 1900).
• Alice Paul — formed Congressional Union; organized "Silent Sentinels" who picketed the White House.
• Western states granted women's suffrage first (Wyoming, Colorado, Idaho, Utah).
• 19th Amendment (1920) — guaranteed women's right to vote nationally.

Key terms: NAWSA Alice Paul Silent Sentinels 19th Amendment
African American Rights: Washington vs. Du Bois
• Booker T. Washington — "Atlanta Compromise" (1895): self-help, vocational education, accommodate White racism in exchange for economic opportunity; founded Tuskegee Institute.
• W.E.B. Du Bois — demanded immediate civil rights; founded Niagara Movement (1905), calling for full political and social equality.
• NAACP (1909) — founded by Du Bois, Ida B. Wells-Barnett, Mary Church Terrell and others after the Springfield, IL race riot (1908). Du Bois edited The Crisis newspaper.

Key terms: Tuskegee Institute Niagara Movement NAACP 1909
TR as President: The Square Deal
• Roosevelt became president when McKinley was assassinated (September 1901, Pan-American Exposition, Buffalo, NY) — youngest president at 42.
• Trust-busting: filed suit against Northern Securities Trust (railroad monopoly, won 1904 5-4); over 44 antitrust suits total. Distinguished "good trusts" vs. "bad trusts."
• Anthracite Coal Strike (1902): TR invited both sides to White House to negotiate — first time a president sided with workers, not owners.
• Square Deal legislation: Meat Inspection Act (1906); Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) — after reading Upton Sinclair's The Jungle.
• Conservation: appointed Gifford Pinchot as first chief of U.S. Forestry Service; created 18 national monuments, 5 national parks, 150+ national forests (~230 million acres).
• Elkins Act (1903) & Hepburn Act (1906): strengthened Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate railroad rates.

Key terms: Square Deal Northern Securities Meat Inspection Act 1906 Pure Food & Drug Act 1906
Taft's Presidency & the 1912 Election
• TR hand-picked William Howard Taft (Secretary of War, former Philippine Governor-General) as his successor; Taft won 1908.
• Taft initiated twice as many antitrust suits as TR, but lacked TR's political finesse. Signed Mann-Elkins Act (1910) extending ICC authority to telephones and telegraphs.
• Problems: Payne-Aldrich Act (1909) raised tariffs; fired Gifford Pinchot (conservation scandal with Sec. Ballinger); supported conservative Republicans. TR felt betrayed.
• 1912 Election: TR bolted GOP to form the Progressive (Bull Moose) Party. Four candidates: TR (Progressive), Taft (Republican), Woodrow Wilson (Democrat), Eugene Debs (Socialist). TR survived assassination attempt in Milwaukee. Wilson won with 435 electoral votes; TR second; Taft third.

Key terms: Payne-Aldrich Act Bull Moose Party New Nationalism (TR) New Freedom (Wilson)
Wilson's New Freedom
• Wilson: former history professor and president of Princeton; Progressive governor of New Jersey.
• New Freedom = smaller federal government protecting public from big business, vs. TR's "New Nationalism" (powerful federal government regulating big business).
• Phase 1 (1913): Underwood Tariff Act — reduced tariffs significantly; established federal income tax (16th Amendment). Federal Reserve Act (1913) — created central banking system.
• Phase 2 (1916, pre-reelection): Clayton Antitrust Act; federal child labor laws; 8-hour workday for railroad workers; Keating-Owen Act.
• WWI (1917) effectively ended the domestic Progressive agenda — national security replaced social reform.

Key terms: Federal Reserve Act 1913 Underwood Tariff Clayton Antitrust Act 16th Amendment
Chapter 22 — Age of Empire: American Foreign Policy (1890–1914)
Turner & Mahan · Spanish-American War · Open Door · Big Stick · Dollar Diplomacy
▾
The Roots of Empire: Turner & Mahan
After the Civil War, the U.S. was isolationist — the State Dept. had barely 60 employees and no ambassadors (1865). Two thinkers changed the direction:
• Frederick Jackson Turner — "Frontier Thesis" (1893): the frontier shaped American democracy and character, but the frontier is now closed. Raised the question: where does America expand next?
• Alfred Thayer Mahan — The Influence of Sea Power upon History: America needed a large steel navy, overseas naval bases (coaling stations), and a Central American canal to become a world power. Naval Act of 1890 followed his advice.
• Seward's Folly (1867): Secretary of State Seward purchased Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million — mocked at the time. Also annexed Midway Islands (crucial coaling station).

Key terms: Frontier Thesis (Turner) Mahan / Sea Power Seward's Folly
Hawaii & Pacific Expansion
• American sugar planters dominated Hawaiian economy; by 1890, Hawaii exported nearly all sugar to the U.S. tariff-free.
• Queen Liliuokalani (1891–1893) tried to reassert Hawaiian sovereignty against American economic dominance. American businessmen worked with U.S. minister John Stevens to stage an armed revolt.
• Hawaii annexed in 1898 during the Spanish-American War; became a territory in 1900.

Key terms: Queen Liliuokalani Hawaii annexation 1898
The Spanish-American War (1898)
• Causes: Cuba's fight for independence from Spain; yellow journalism (Hearst, Pulitzer) inflamed American public; the mysterious sinking of the USS Maine in Havana Harbor (Feb 1898) — "Remember the Maine!"
• Teller Amendment — U.S. promised Cuba independence after the war (no annexation).
• The war lasted ~4 months. TR's Rough Riders fought at San Juan Hill, Cuba. The U.S. Navy destroyed the Spanish fleet in both the Caribbean and the Pacific.
• Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898): Spain recognized Cuba's independence; U.S. gained Puerto Rico, Guam, and Philippines ($20 million paid to Spain).
• Anti-Imperialist League (1898): prominent opponents of empire — Jane Addams, Andrew Carnegie, Mark Twain, Samuel Gompers. Senate barely ratified the treaty (when Filipino uprising news arrived, undecided senators voted yes).
• Platt Amendment — gave U.S. right to intervene in Cuban affairs; secured Guantanamo Bay naval base. Cuba was "independent" in name only.

Key terms: Rough Riders Treaty of Paris 1898 Teller Amendment Platt Amendment Anti-Imperialist League yellow journalism
The Philippine-American War
• Filipinos, led by Emilio Aguinaldo, expected independence after the Spanish-American War — instead the U.S. kept the Philippines.
• The Philippine-American War lasted 3 years: ~4,000 Americans killed, ~20,000 Filipino combatants, and an estimated 250,000 civilian deaths.
• Taft Commission: William Howard Taft appointed civil governor; built infrastructure, hospitals, schools; offered political positions to former rebels. Aguinaldo captured in 1901 and forced to swear allegiance. Philippines remained under U.S. control until 1946.

Key terms: Emilio Aguinaldo Taft Commission
Open Door Policy in China
• European powers (Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan) carved out spheres of influence in China, giving each exclusive trade advantages in their zones.
• Secretary of State John Hay issued Open Door notes (1899): circular notes demanding equal trade access to all Chinese ports for all nations, no special tariffs, no transportation controls.
• This benefited the U.S. the most — American goods were higher quality and cheaper; "open doors" flooded China with U.S. products. Other nations gave half-hearted replies, but Hay declared it official U.S. policy.
• Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901): Chinese insurgents (Righteous and Harmonious Fists) tried to expel all western influence. U.S., Britain, and Germany sent ~2,000 troops; crushed the rebellion; reinforced U.S. commitment to China's territorial integrity.

Key terms: Open Door notes (Hay) sphere of influence Boxer Rebellion
Roosevelt's Big Stick Foreign Policy
• Based on an African proverb: "Speak softly and carry a big stick, and you will go far."
• Threaten force rather than actually use it — keep opponents guessing. U.S. military credibility after Spanish-American War made threats believable.
• Panama Canal: Colombia rejected TR's treaty offer ($10M + $250K/yr). TR secretly encouraged Panamanian revolution (1903) — sent battleships to block Colombia. Recognized Panama immediately; signed canal deal with new nation. Canal opened in 1914. TR became first sitting president to travel internationally (visited construction site).
• Roosevelt Corollary (1904): extension of Monroe Doctrine — U.S. would act as "international police power" in Latin America to correct any "chronic wrongdoing." Gave U.S. right to intervene in Latin American affairs to maintain stability (and protect American interests). Established protectorates over Cuba, Panama, Dominican Republic.
• Russo-Japanese War: TR mediated (Portsmouth, NH, 1905); won Nobel Peace Prize — but Japanese felt cheated out of reparations; tensions with Japan began.

Key terms: Big Stick diplomacy Panama Canal 1914 Roosevelt Corollary 1904 Monroe Doctrine
Taft's Dollar Diplomacy
• Taft's approach: "substitute dollars for bullets" — use American economic power (not military threats) to achieve foreign policy goals.
• Central America: paid off debts these nations owed European powers with U.S. dollars — making them indebted to the U.S. instead. When Nicaragua refused U.S. loans, Taft sent warships.
• Lodge Corollary: addendum to Roosevelt Corollary — no foreign corporation (other than American) could acquire strategic lands in the Western Hemisphere. (Triggered by Japan's interest in Mexican territory.)
• Asia: tried to bolster China against Japan; efforts backfired and heightened U.S.-Japan tensions — contributing to WWII.
• Long-term damage: created resentment, economic instability, and nationalist movements in Central America; alienated Japan.

Key terms: Dollar Diplomacy Lodge Corollary
Chapter 23 — Americans and the Great War (1914–1919)
Isolationism · U-boats · Home Front · 14 Points · Versailles · Red Scare
▾
Origins of WWI & American Neutrality
• WWI began in Europe in 1914 — sparked by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (Austro-Hungarian heir) in Sarajevo, June 28, 1914.
• Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire.
• Allied Powers: Britain, France, Russia; later Italy and Japan.
• Wilson declared neutrality — wanted to keep U.S. out; strong ethnic ties to both sides made public opinion divided; Wilson's SoS Bryan was a committed anti-imperialist.
• Wilson proposed an idealistic moral foreign policy — he would intervene only when morally imperative. In practice, he still intervened in Mexico, Haiti, Dominican Republic.
• Problem: free trade with the Allies conflicted with German submarine warfare, which targeted all shipping to Britain.

Key terms: neutrality Central Powers Allied Powers
Events Forcing U.S. Into War
• German U-boats (submarines) — Germany declared a war zone around Britain; submarines attacked ships without warning, violating international law (ships were supposed to warn and allow evacuation).
• Sinking of the Lusitania (May 7, 1915): British ocean liner torpedoed; 1,198 killed including 128 Americans. Public outrage, but Wilson still stayed out of war. Germany agreed to Sussex Pledge (1916) — limit submarine warfare.
• Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917): German Foreign Minister Arthur Zimmermann secretly offered Mexico an alliance if U.S. entered war — Mexico could reclaim Texas, New Mexico, Arizona. British intercepted and shared it with the U.S. Public outrage.
• Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1917), directly threatening American ships.
• Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war — April 6, 1917. 56 members of Congress voted against it.

Key terms: Lusitania 1915 Zimmermann Telegram 1917 U-boats / unrestricted submarine warfare
Preparing for War
• Selective Service Act (1917): military draft — eventually 4 million men served.
• Committee on Public Information (Creel Committee): George Creel directed massive propaganda campaign against Germany — posters, pamphlets, films depicting Germany as brutal; "Four-Minute Men" gave short pro-war speeches at movie theaters.
• Liberty bonds: Americans urged to buy war bonds to finance the war.
• War Industries Board, Food Administration, Fuel Administration: federal agencies coordinating wartime production and rationing.

Key terms: Creel Committee / CPI Selective Service Act liberty bonds
The Home Front: Suppression of Dissent & Anti-German Sentiment
• Espionage Act (1917): prohibited aiding the enemy; banned public opposition to the war; up to 20-year prison sentences.
• Sedition Act (1918): prohibited any criticism of the government, military, Constitution, or American flag. Over 2,000 charged.
• Eugene Debs sentenced to 10 years for encouraging draft resistance.
• Anti-German hysteria: sauerkraut renamed "liberty cabbage"; German-Americans faced discrimination; breweries targeted (mostly German-owned) — accelerated Prohibition.
• Schenck v. United States (1919): Supreme Court (Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes) unanimously upheld Espionage/Sedition Acts — speech that presents a "clear and present danger" is not protected. (Holmes compared it to falsely shouting "fire!" in a crowded theater.)
• American Protective League: private citizens aided DOJ in spying on suspected dissenters and draft evaders.

Key terms: Espionage Act 1917 Sedition Act 1918 Schenck v. U.S. "clear and present danger"
The Home Front: New Opportunities
• Labor: National Labor War Board (1918) — in exchange for AFL's "no-strike pledge," government protected workers' right to organize and bargain. Union membership jumped from 2.6M (1916) to 4.1M (1919). But wages were offset by 15–20% annual inflation.
• Women: Over 1 million entered workforce for first time; 8M+ found higher-paying jobs in industry (railroads tripled). "Farmerettes" (Women's Land Army) ran farms. ~30,000 women served in military/humanitarian roles (Red Cross nurses; "Hello Girls" — bilingual telephone operators in France). Wartime service built case for 19th Amendment (1920).
• African Americans: 350,000 Black men served; segregated units. Harlem Hellfighters (369th Infantry) served on French frontline for 6 months — longer than any other American unit; 171 received Legion of Merit. Great Migration accelerated: ~350,000 African Americans moved north from 1910–1920.

Key terms: Harlem Hellfighters / 369th Infantry Great Migration no-strike pledge
The War & American Military
• American Expeditionary Forces (AEF) under General John "Black Jack" Pershing arrived in France 1917.
• Meuse-Argonne Offensive (September 1918): over 1 million U.S. troops; 40 days of intense fighting; broke German lines.
• Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated November 9, 1918. Armistice: November 11, 1918.
• American toll: 117,000 killed, 206,000 wounded. Allied total: 5.7 million military deaths. Central Powers: 4 million. War cost U.S. over $32 billion.

Key terms: AEF / Pershing Armistice November 11, 1918 Meuse-Argonne Offensive
Wilson's 14 Points & the Treaty of Versailles
• Wilson announced his Fourteen Points (January 1918) — a vision for post-war peace: free trade, freedom of seas, no secret treaties, self-determination for nations, and a League of Nations.
• Wilson attended Paris Peace Conference personally (Dec 1918–June 1919) — unprecedented; other nations followed. Britain, France, Italy sought German reparations and territorial gains. Japan sought concessions in Asia.
• Treaty of Versailles (1919): far from Wilson's 14 Points. Germany forced to accept "war guilt clause" (sole responsibility for WWI), pay over $33 billion in reparations, surrender territories, disarm military. League of Nations included but without U.S.
• Senate battle: Republicans divided into Irreconcilables (opposed treaty entirely) and Reservationists (like Henry Cabot Lodge) who would support it with amendments removing Article X. Wilson refused to compromise; went on cross-country speaking tour; suffered debilitating stroke September 1919. His wife Edith Wilson effectively ran the presidency for ~6 months.
• Senate voted down the treaty twice — U.S. never joined League of Nations; technically remained at war with Germany until Knox-Porter Resolution (July 1921).

Key terms: Fourteen Points League of Nations war guilt clause Treaty of Versailles Irreconcilables Reservationists
Difficult Aftermath: Red Scare, Red Summer & Harding
• Flu Pandemic (1918–1919): 675,000 Americans died; ~500 million infected worldwide; 50 million deaths globally. Added to postwar trauma.
• Red Scare: fear of Communist/Bolshevik revolution in the U.S. after Russia's 1917 revolution. Letter bombs found (1919); "Palmer Raids" — Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer and J. Edgar Hoover arrested ~4,000 alleged radicals; ~250 deported on "Soviet Ark."
• Red Summer (1919): 25+ bloody race riots in northern cities; 250+ killed. Chicago Race Riot: triggered when White mob stoned a Black teen who swam near "White beach" on Lake Michigan; police didn't arrest the killer. Week-long riot, 38 dead.
• Tulsa Massacre (1921): White mob destroyed Greenwood ("Black Wall Street") district; estimates of 50–300 Black deaths; omitted from history for decades.
• Election of 1920: Warren G. Harding (Ohio Republican) ran on "return to normalcy" — won in landslide (404 electoral votes, 60% popular vote). Americans exhausted by Progressive mandates, war, flu, racial violence, economic anxiety, Red Scare.

Key terms: Red Scare Red Summer 1919 Palmer Raids / J. Edgar Hoover Chicago Race Riot 1919 Tulsa Massacre 1921 "return to normalcy" (Harding)

CH.21
What was the Progressive Era? Describe its key features and goals. Who were the muckrakers, and what role did they play in launching the movement?
Check what you covered:
The Progressive Era (approximately 1890–1920) was a broad reform movement that emerged in reaction to the social ills of the Gilded Age. As rapid industrialization created extreme wealth inequality, dangerous working conditions, corrupt machine politics, monopolistic corporations, and surging racial violence, a coalition of reformers coalesced around three core goals: expanding democracy (making government more directly accountable to citizens), improving efficiency (replacing corrupt political machines with expert management), and advancing social justice (addressing poverty, child labor, alcohol abuse, and workplace safety). Most Progressives were educated, middle-class Anglo-Saxon Protestants, but the movement was diverse, including women, African Americans, immigrants, and rural communities. At the center of the Progressive awakening were the muckrakers — investigative journalists who, unlike the sensationalist "yellow journalists," exposed specific social problems with the explicit goal of spurring public action. Ida Tarbell published a damning series on Standard Oil's monopolistic practices. Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle depicted gruesome conditions in Chicago meatpacking plants. Lincoln Steffens documented corruption in city government. Jacob Riis photographed the squalid conditions in New York's tenement slums. By bringing these problems into middle-class living rooms, muckrakers created the public will for reform that politicians then acted upon.
CH.21
Evaluate Theodore Roosevelt as a Progressive president. How did his "Square Deal" advance the Progressive agenda through trust-busting, labor disputes, food and drug safety, and land conservation?
Check what you covered:
Theodore Roosevelt became the nation's youngest president at 42 not through election but through the assassination of William McKinley in September 1901. Republican Party bosses had placed him on the ticket as vice president specifically to neutralize his progressive influence — a plan that dramatically backfired. Roosevelt's "Square Deal" represented a transformative vision of the federal government as an active steward of the public good, in stark contrast to the hands-off Gilded Age precedent. On trust-busting, Roosevelt filed suit against the Northern Securities Trust in 1902 — a railroad conglomerate controlled by Rockefeller and J.P. Morgan — and won a 5-4 Supreme Court ruling in 1904. He ultimately filed over 44 antitrust suits, though he distinguished "good trusts" (efficient, benefiting consumers) from "bad trusts" (exploitative monopolies) rather than opposing all large corporations. During the Anthracite Coal Strike of 1902, Roosevelt invited both the mine owners and union to the White House and pressed for a compromise — the first time a president sided with workers rather than automatically deploying troops against them. Responding to public outrage over Upton Sinclair's exposé of the meatpacking industry, TR pushed through both the Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug Act (1906), establishing federal oversight of food safety and mandatory labeling. Finally, his conservation legacy was unprecedented: 230 million acres of public land protected through national parks, monuments, and forests, overseen by his appointed Forestry Service chief, Gifford Pinchot.
CH.22
Describe the Spanish-American War (1898). What caused it, what happened, and what territories did the U.S. acquire? How did the war mark a turning point in U.S. foreign policy?
Check what you covered:
The Spanish-American War (1898) was a pivotal turning point that launched the United States as a global imperial power. The war grew from a combination of genuine sympathy for Cuba's independence struggle against Spain, sensationalist "yellow journalism" by William Randolph Hearst and Joseph Pulitzer that inflamed public opinion, and the mysterious explosion of the USS Maine in Havana Harbor in February 1898, which killed 266 American sailors. The Teller Amendment, passed before the war, pledged that the U.S. would not annex Cuba. The war itself lasted only about four months. Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders fought in Cuba at San Juan Hill, while the U.S. Navy demolished the Spanish fleet in both the Caribbean and Manila Bay in the Philippines. The Treaty of Paris (December 10, 1898) recognized Cuba's independence and transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States for $20 million. The Anti-Imperialist League — whose members included Andrew Carnegie, Mark Twain, Jane Addams, and Samuel Gompers — opposed empire building as contrary to American democratic principles. The Senate nearly defeated the treaty, but news of Filipino resistance convinced undecided senators to ratify it. The Platt Amendment subsequently gave the U.S. the right to intervene in Cuban affairs at will and secured a permanent naval base at Guantanamo Bay, making Cuban independence largely nominal. In the Philippines, a brutal three-year war against Emilio Aguinaldo's independence movement (1899–1902) killed 4,000 Americans and as many as 250,000 Filipino civilians before the U.S. established control that would last until 1946.
CH.23
Discuss Wilson's Fourteen Points and the Treaty of Versailles. To what extent did the treaty reflect Wilson's vision? How was it punitive toward Germany, and why did the U.S. Senate reject it?
Check what you covered:
Wilson announced his Fourteen Points in January 1918, months before the armistice, establishing an ambitious vision for postwar order: free trade, freedom of the seas, an end to secret treaties, the right of self-determination for all peoples, and most importantly, a League of Nations that would resolve international disputes through negotiation rather than war. Wilson made the unprecedented decision to attend the Paris Peace Conference personally, which other leaders followed. But there he encountered a stark reality: Britain, France, and Italy — exhausted, humiliated, and having suffered catastrophic losses — wanted Germany punished, not pardoned. The resulting Treaty of Versailles bore little resemblance to Wilson's original vision. Germany was forced to accept the "war guilt clause" (Article 231), taking sole responsibility for starting the war, and was compelled to pay reparations exceeding $33 billion to the Allies. Germany lost significant territory and was required to drastically disarm. The League of Nations was included, but stripped of much of Wilson's envisioned authority. Back home, the Senate was deeply divided. Irreconcilables opposed the treaty entirely on isolationist grounds. Reservationists, led by Republican Henry Cabot Lodge, would accept it if Article X (the League's collective security provision) were amended to preserve Congressional war powers. Wilson stubbornly refused all compromise, embarked on a grueling speaking tour, and collapsed after a public event in September 1919. He suffered a debilitating stroke; his wife Edith informally ran the government for months. The Senate voted down the treaty twice, and the U.S. never joined the League of Nations. The harsh reparations burden imposed on Germany would fuel economic collapse, national resentment, and ultimately the conditions that brought Hitler to power and led to World War II.
CH.23
Describe the difficult aftermath of WWI in America. Explain the Red Scare, Red Summer of 1919, the flu pandemic, and how these factors contributed to Harding's landslide victory in 1920.
Check what you covered:
The end of World War I brought not celebration but a cascade of crises that shook American society to its foundations. The returning troops brought with them a devastating new influenza strain; by the spring of 1919, approximately 675,000 Americans had died — more than the war had killed — with an estimated 50 million deaths worldwide. Economic disruption followed the end of wartime production: inflation ran 15–20% annually from 1917–1920, and the cost of living nearly doubled by 1919. Workers, freed from their wartime no-strike pledge, launched an unprecedented wave of labor action — more than four million workers participated in nearly three thousand strikes in 1919 alone, both records in American history. Simultaneously, the Bolshevik Revolution in Russia triggered a Red Scare at home. When letter bombs were discovered addressed to prominent officials and explosions damaged the home of Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer in 1919, the government cracked down. Palmer and his assistant J. Edgar Hoover organized raids in twelve cities, arresting approximately 4,000 alleged radicals and deporting around 250 on what newspapers called the "Soviet Ark." Race violence erupted as well. During the "Red Summer" of 1919, twenty-five bloody race riots killed more than 250 people. The Chicago Race Riot was triggered when a White mob stoned a Black teenager who drifted near the "White beach" on Lake Michigan — police at the scene did not arrest the attacker. A week of rioting followed, killing 38. The Tulsa Massacre of 1921 was even deadlier, as a White mob systematically destroyed the prosperous Greenwood district. Exhausted by war, Progressive government interventions, racial violence, economic anxiety, and fear of Communist infiltrators, Americans voted overwhelmingly for Senator Warren G. Harding, who promised a "return to normalcy." Harding won 404 electoral votes and 60% of the popular vote — a decisive mandate to step back from the world and Progressive activism alike.
