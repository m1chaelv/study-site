LO 7.1 What interest groups are & why they form ▼

Explain what interest groups are, why they form, and their essential characteristics.

Key concepts: An interest group (also called a pressure group, special interest group, or lobby) is an organization whose members share common views/objectives and try to influence government officials and policy for the group's benefit. Lobbying = communicating directly with an official to influence a policy decision. Interest groups differ from political parties: parties want to WIN office (get their own members elected/appointed and control government); interest groups want to INFLUENCE officials of any party, without necessarily running their own members. They provide functional representation (by shared interest) that supplements the geographic representation of elected office. Why they form: (1) legal & cultural reasons — the First Amendment right of association, recognized in NAACP v. Alabama (1958), plus a culture of joining groups; (2) a decentralized government — federalism + separation of powers create many access points; (3) the strength or weakness of the party system and political ideologies — weak parties = stronger interest groups. Characteristics: members are mostly from professional/managerial occupations with above-average income/education; every group has an active minority and a passive majority (a few make the decisions); a free rider gets the group's benefits without joining or paying.

LO 7.2 Types of interest groups & what makes one powerful ▼

Describe types of interest groups and analyze the traits of a powerful interest group.

Key concepts — the four broad types: (1) Economic interest groups — the most common in Texas; promote members' economic self-interest. Includes business organizations (often trade associations; the Texas Association of Business is an umbrella organization that coordinates smaller groups) and labor organizations (unions — relatively weak in Texas, under 5% membership). (2) Professional / public employee groups — licensing and standards (State Bar of Texas, the powerful Texas Medical Association). (3) Social interest groups — racial/ethnic (NAACP; LULAC, the oldest Latino group, founded 1929; MALDEF), women's (League of Women Voters, Annie's List), and religion-based groups (the Christian Coalition is one of the state's most influential; also Texas Values, Texas Freedom Network). (4) Public interest groups — claim to promote the general good, not a narrow private interest (Common Cause, League of Conservation Voters, Sierra Club). Power groups: business trade associations, professional associations (doctors, lawyers, teachers), and labor unions. Traits of a powerful group: sizable membership, above-average money, knowledgeable/dedicated leadership, and high unity; plus strong links to legislators and bureaucrats, status as a repeat player, and an Austin headquarters. In Texas, business groups are generally more powerful than labor.

LO 7.3 Techniques interest groups use to influence government ▼

Evaluate the activities interest groups use to influence Texas government.

Key concepts — the techniques: (1) Lobbying — the oldest, best-known tactic; Texas ranks 2nd (after California) in money spent lobbying, and lobbyists outnumber the 181 legislators about 9 to 1. (2) Personal communication — a lobbyist's main tool is information/expertise delivered to officials. (3) Favors and gifts — capped: no more than $500/year in gifts from a single lobbyist, gifts over $250 must be reported, and a $300/year cap on food/drink (from the 1991 law). (4) Grassroots activities — mobilizing members/sympathizers to create an image of broad support; "astroturfing"/"astrotweeting" uses fake social-media accounts to fake that support. (5) Electioneering — participating in campaigns (endorsements, mailing/email lists, "Get Out the Vote"). (6) Campaign financing through PACs. (7) rarely, bribery/unethical practices (e.g., the Sharpstown scandal). A PAC is "a group of persons that has a principal purpose of accepting political contributions or making political contributions." Texas sets no limits on PAC/individual contributions except in judicial races, and bans contributions during the regular session (a Dec–June moratorium). Super PACs (after Citizens United) raise unlimited sums for independent spending.

LO 7.4 How interest groups are regulated & how well it works ▼

Assess how interest groups are regulated and evaluate the effectiveness of those laws.

Key concepts: Prompted by media reports of heavy lobby spending and a grand-jury investigation, in 1991 the 72nd Legislature proposed a constitutional amendment — approved by voters that November — creating the eight-member Texas Ethics Commission (TEC) to enforce legal standards for lobbyists and public officials. (On your review this is the "1991 Ethics Act"; the textbook covers the substance without using that exact label.) Appointments: governor (4), lieutenant governor (2), Speaker (2). The law expanded disclosure, put a $300/year cap on lobbyist-provided food/drink, banned honoraria and lobby-paid pleasure trips, and required disclosure of gifts over $250. TEC duties: collect campaign-finance disclosure reports (filed 2–7 times a year), hear ethics complaints, and assess fines. Weaknesses: small budget/staff; lobbyists report only compensation ranges, not exact dollars; dark/secret money (undisclosed donors) is growing; and the TEC must dismiss an Election Code complaint if the respondent calls the violation a clerical error and fixes it within two weeks. Texas is unusual for a big state: strong interest groups, weak parties — a product of its individualistic/traditionalistic political culture, long one-party history, and the weak, fragmented government created by the 1876 Constitution.

LO 8.1 Structure of the Texas legislature ▼

Describe the structure of the Texas legislature.

Key concepts: The legislature is bicameral (two houses): the larger House of Representatives (150 members) plus the smaller Senate (31 members) = 181 legislators total. (Only Nebraska is unicameral.) Terms: Representatives serve 2 years; Senators serve 4 years, staggered (about half elected every two years). Terms begin in January of odd-numbered years. Regular session: begins the second Tuesday in January of odd-numbered years, runs 140 days, and is biennial (every two years). Special session: called by the governor, lasts no more than 30 days each, with no limit on the number; the governor sets the agenda (legislators may consider only what the governor puts before them). Redistricting happens in the first odd-numbered year of a decade, after the federal census. Reapportionment = allotting seats by population; redistricting = drawing the new lines. If the legislature fails, the Legislative Redistricting Board (5 ex officio members: lt. gov, Speaker, attorney general, comptroller, land commissioner) steps in. Gerrymandering = drawing districts to favor a group or party (tactics: packing, cracking, pairing; the term comes from Elbridge Gerry, 1812). Single-member districts (one member per district — used for all Texas House and Senate seats since 1975) lower campaign costs and elect more Black and Latino candidates than multimember districts did. Reynolds v. Sims (1964) established "one person, one vote."

LO 8.2 Membership: qualifications, characteristics, pay ▼

Describe the membership of the Texas legislature — qualifications and compensation.

Key concepts — formal (constitutional) qualifications: House — age 21+, U.S. & Texas citizen, qualified voter, Texas resident 2 years, district resident 1 year. Senate — age 26+, Texas resident 5 years, district resident 1 year. Legislators may not hold another government office. Informal qualifications (the typical legislator): White, male, middle-aged (40–60), college-educated, an attorney or businessperson, and an incumbent (Catholics are the most numerous religious group, followed by Baptists). Descriptive representation = the lawmaker shares constituents' characteristics (a Latina representing Latinos); substantive representation = the lawmaker works for a group's interests regardless of shared traits. Research: increasing descriptive representation for a group improves substantive representation for that group. Compensation: $600/month = $7,200/year, set in the constitution and unchanged since 1975 (changeable only by amendment/voter approval); plus a per diem ($221/day in 2021, set by the Ethics Commission) and a relatively generous pension. Texas is one of 35 states with no term limits.

LO 8.3 Organization: presiding officers, committees, caucuses ▼

Compare the organization of the Texas House and Senate.

Key concepts — presiding officers: The Lieutenant Governor is President of the Senate (but is NOT a senator; elected statewide; usually the most powerful state officer when the legislature is in session — most of the power comes from Senate Rules, not the constitution). The president pro tempore presides when the lt. gov is absent. The Speaker of the House is elected by House members in an open vote to a two-year term and is generally the second most powerful official. Both presiding officers appoint committee members and chairs, refer bills to committee, control floor action, and break ties. Committees: substantive (consider bills by subject, e.g., Agriculture); procedural (internal matters, e.g., Calendars); select (temporary, cross-jurisdictional, may include non-legislators); standing (permanent); interim / special interim (study issues between sessions); and conference committee (reconciles differing House/Senate versions of a bill — five members from each chamber). A caucus is a group of legislators organized by party, ideology, race/ethnicity, or issue; caucuses must register with the Texas Ethics Commission (e.g., the Legislative Black Caucus is a racial/ethnic caucus).

LO 8.4 Responsibilities: bills, resolutions, oversight, impeachment ▼

Outline the responsibilities of the Texas legislature.

Key concepts: The legislature is the principal agent in making public policy. A bill is a proposed law (HB or SB) before enactment; its three categories are special (an exception for a specific person/class/corporation), general (applies to all of Texas), and local (a single unit of local government). To become law, a bill needs a simple majority in both chambers (two-thirds for an emergency measure taking immediate effect). Resolutions: a simple resolution involves one chamber only and is NOT sent to the governor (internal matters); a concurrent resolution passes both chambers and IS sent to the governor (e.g., adjournment); a joint resolution passes both chambers and is used to propose constitutional amendments and to ratify U.S. constitutional amendments (not signed by the governor). How a bill becomes law (House path): introduction (filed electronically with the chief clerk) → first reading & referral to committee by the Speaker → committee report → second reading, debate & vote → third reading & final passage → the Senate repeats the steps → conference committee if the versions differ → enrollment & signatures → the governor signs, lets it become law unsigned, or vetoes. Oversight = requiring reports from state agencies (tools include the state auditor via the Legislative Audit Committee, the Sunset Advisory Commission that reviews agencies every 12 years, and senatorial courtesy). Most gubernatorial board/commission appointments need approval by two-thirds of senators present. Impeachment: the House impeaches (simple majority present); the Senate convicts (two-thirds of membership); the Chief Justice of the Texas Supreme Court presides. Delaying tactics: a filibuster (Senate — speak as long as endurance permits) and chubbing (House — lengthy debate on buffer bills to run out the clock). Ghost voting = pressing another member's voting button (prohibited without permission). Veto: in session, overridden by two-thirds of both chambers; a post-adjournment veto (within 20 days of adjournment) cannot be overridden.

⚠️ Pocket veto: on your review, but Texas has no pocket veto. Federally, a pocket veto lets an executive kill a bill by taking no action while the legislature is adjourned so it can't override. In Texas the opposite happens — an unsigned bill becomes law (after 10 days in session, or 20 days after adjournment). The closest Texas analog is the post-adjournment veto (an active veto after sine die that can't be overridden). Not in any of your materials — sourced externally, verified against Texas Government 2.0 / OERTX.
LO 8.5 Influences on legislators' voting decisions ▼

Explain the influences on legislators' voting decisions.

Key concepts: In theory, constituents (especially voters) have the most influence, because they elect legislators. In practice, lawmakers are heavily swayed by the presiding officers, the governor (a threatened veto can kill a bill), the attorney general and comptroller, and lobbyists. Research organizations also shape decisions: the nonpartisan Texas Legislative Council (drafts bills, runs the "Red Apple" redistricting software); the House Research Organization (HRO) — bipartisan, produces the Daily Floor Report with arguments for and against each bill; the Senate Research Center; Every Texan (formerly the Center for Public Policy Priorities — critics call it too liberal); and the Texas Public Policy Foundation (TPPF) — conservative, described as the "in-house think tank of the state's current Republican leadership." The media (Texas Tribune, Quorum Report) round out the influences.

🏛️ Chapter 7: The Politics of Interest Groups▼

LO 7.1 — What Interest Groups Are & Why They Form

  • Interest group — an organization whose members share common views/objectives and participate in activities to influence government officials and policy decisions for the benefit of the group or its cause. Also called a pressure group, special interest group, or lobby.
  • Primary objective of an interest group: to influence government officials and their policies on behalf of the group's members (not to place its own members in office, and not necessarily to serve the general public).
  • Lobbying — when a group or individual communicates directly with a government official to influence that official's decision on a policy matter.
  • Interest group vs. political party (a classic exam contrast): a party's main purpose is to get its members elected/appointed and control government; an interest group seeks to influence officials of any party to its advantage, usually without running its own members for office. Interest groups add functional representation (representation by shared interest) on top of the geographic representation of elected office.
  • Right of association — the constitutional foundation for forming interest groups. In NAACP v. Alabama (1958) the U.S. Supreme Court recognized the right of association as part of the First Amendment right of assembly. 📌 Review asks: "Which constitutional right is the foundation of the right to form interest groups?" → the First Amendment right of association.
  • Why interest groups form/grow: (1) Legal & cultural reasons — the right of association plus an American culture of joining associations; (2) Decentralized government — federalism (national/state/local) plus separation of powers (legislative/executive/judicial) creates many access points to fight battles at different levels/branches; (3) Strength or weakness of the party system + ideologies — weak, undisciplined parties make officials more vulnerable to organized interest-group pressure.
  • Characteristics/membership: members come chiefly from professional and managerial occupations with above-average income and education. Every organized group has an active minority and a passive majority — decisions are made by relatively few (leaders with money, time, and motivation).
  • Free rider — someone who receives the benefits an interest group wins without joining or paying the costs of membership (the teacher who doesn't join the union but still gets the raise the union lobbied for).

LO 7.2 — Types of Interest Groups & Power

  • Economic interest groups — the most common type in Texas; exist to promote members' economic self-interest (maximize profits/wages).
    • Business organizations — advocate lower taxes and fewer regulations; often take the form of trade associations (act on behalf of an industry). The Texas Association of Business (TAB) is an umbrella organization — it represents smaller groups and coordinates their activities. 📌 Review: "What type of group would most support the Texas right-to-work law?" → business/employer organizations support it; labor unions oppose it. ⚠️ "Right-to-work" was dropped from the current edition but is defined in the older edition — a law that limits the power of workers/unions to bargain (Texas enacted its right-to-work law in 1947; it bans the union shop).
    • Labor organizations (unions) — seek higher wages, health/unemployment insurance, safe conditions. Relatively weak in Texas — under 5% union membership. Business groups are generally more powerful than labor here.
  • Professional / public employee groups — concerned with licensing and standards of a profession (State Bar of Texas, Texas Society of CPAs, and the Texas Medical Association, a powerful professional group). Public officer/employee groups (e.g., Texas Public Employees Association) push for pay and benefits.
  • Social interest groups — organized around a shared identity or cause rather than economics:
    • Racial/ethnic: NAACP; LULAC (League of United Latin American Citizens) — the oldest Latino group in Texas, founded 1929 in Corpus Christi; MALDEF (litigation for Latino rights, founded 1968).
    • Women's: League of Women Voters (nonpartisan), Texas Federation of Republican Women (partisan), Annie's List (recruits progressive women).
    • Religion-based groups: the Christian Coalition — continues to be one of the state's most influential political forces (peaked in the 1990s). Also Texas Values / Texas Values Action (conservative religious-liberty advocacy) and the Texas Freedom Network (formed to counter the Christian Coalition).
  • Public interest groups — claim to promote the general interest of society, not a narrow private/corporate interest. Common Cause Texas (government reform), the Texas League of Conservation Voters (scores lawmakers on "green" bills), Sierra Club, MADD.
  • Texas power groups — the interest groups legislators consider most powerful: business trade associations (oil, gas, insurance, etc.), professional associations (physicians, lawyers, teachers), and labor unions.
  • What makes a group powerful (its "necessary factors"): a sizable membership, above-average financial resources, knowledgeable and dedicated leadership, and a high degree of unity; plus strong links to both legislators and bureaucrats, status as a repeat player, and a headquarters in Austin. (Note: the single best "necessary factor" the sample quiz highlights is knowledgeable and dedicated leadership.)

LO 7.3 — Interest Group Techniques

  • Lobbying — the oldest and best-known technique. A lobbyist is an individual hired by an interest group to influence government decision-makers, mostly legislators. Texas ranks 2nd in the nation (after California) in money spent lobbying state government, and lobbyists outnumber the 181 legislators roughly 9 to 1. A group like the Texas Motorcycle Rights Association travels to Austin for a "lobby day" to talk with lawmakers — that's lobbying.
  • Personal communication — a lobbyist's core tool is the information, research, and expertise conveyed to busy officials (compared in the text to a pharmaceutical rep explaining new drugs to doctors).
  • Favors and gifts — meals, tickets, gifts. Limits: no more than $500/year in gifts to a legislator from a single lobbyist; public officials must report gifts over $250; and the 1991 law placed a $300/year cap on lobbyist-provided food and drink and banned lobby-paid pleasure trips.
  • Grassroots activities — mobilizing members and sympathizers to create an image of broad public support (petitions, social media, letters). "Astroturfing" / "astrotweeting" uses fake social-media accounts to create a false impression of how much support (or opposition) an issue has.
  • Electioneering — participating in campaign activity: publicizing incumbents' records, giving favored candidates the group's mailing/email lists, public endorsements, and "Get Out the Vote" drives. (Sample quiz: providing candidates with membership info and mailing/email lists = electioneering.)
  • Campaign financing by PACs. A political action committee (PAC) = "a group of persons that has a principal purpose of accepting political contributions or making political contributions." Texas imposes no limits on how much PACs (or individuals) can give to state candidates except in judicial races. State law bars legislators/statewide officeholders from accepting contributions during a regular session (a moratorium from December to June). Groups make last-minute ("late-train") contributions partly because gifts in the final two days need not be disclosed until the next semiannual report.
  • Super PACs — after Citizens United, these can raise unlimited sums from corporations, unions, associations, and individuals for independent expenditures.
  • Bribery/unethical practices — rare but real (the 1970s Sharpstown bank scandal brought down House Speaker Gus Mutscher and reshaped Texas ethics law).

LO 7.4 — Regulation of Interest Groups

  • The Texas Ethics Commission (TEC) — an eight-member commission created to enforce legal standards for lobbyists and public officials. It is the primary state agency regulating political contributions and expenditures by lobbyists and officials, and it is where ethics complaints against state officials, candidates, and employees are brought. Appointments: governor (4), lieutenant governor (2), Speaker (2).
  • The 1991 ethics reform ("1991 Ethics Act") — in 1991, in response to media reports of big lobby spending and a grand-jury investigation, the 72nd Legislature proposed a constitutional amendment (approved by voters that November) creating the TEC. The law: expanded disclosure requirements; put a $300/year cap on lobbyist-provided food/drink; banned honoraria and lobby-paid pleasure trips; and required disclosure of gifts over $250. 📌 Review: "In response to big spending by lobbyists, what did Texas voters create in 1991?" → the Texas Ethics Commission (via a constitutional amendment).
  • Does Texas have strict finance laws? Not really. Texas campaign-finance law is best described as public disclosure rather than strict limits — the state caps contribution amounts only for judicial candidates and otherwise relies on disclosure. Detailed contribution/expenditure records are filed with the TEC 2 to 7 times a year and posted publicly.
  • Weaknesses of regulation: the TEC has a small budget/staff; lobbyists report only compensation ranges, not exact amounts; dark/secret money (undisclosed nonprofit donors) is growing; and the TEC must dismiss an Election Code complaint if the respondent claims a clerical error and corrects it within two weeks. Many observers say the system still favors incumbents.
  • Why Texas has strong interest groups & weak parties (unusual for a large, wealthy, industrial state): its individualistic and traditionalistic political culture, its long one-party history (first Democratic, then Republican) that limits party competition, and the weak, fragmented government created by the 1876 Constitution.
⚖️ Chapter 8: The Legislative Branch▼

LO 8.1 — Structure, Sessions & Redistricting

  • Bicameral = two chambers. In Texas (and 40 other states) the larger chamber is the House of Representatives. Sizes: House = 150 members, Senate = 31 members → 181 legislators total. Formula to remember: 150 + 31 = 181. (Only Nebraska is unicameral.)
  • Terms: Representatives = 2 years; Senators = 4 years (staggered — about half the Senate is elected every two years). Terms begin in January of odd-numbered years. After redistricting, all 31 senators run at once, then draw lots: 16 get 4-year terms, 15 get 2-year terms, to re-stagger the chamber.
  • Regular session — begins the second Tuesday in January of odd-numbered years, lasts 140 days, and meets biennially (every two years). Texas is one of only four states with biennial sessions.
  • Special session — called by the governor; lasts no more than 30 days each; no limit on the number a governor can call. The governor sets the agenda — the legislature may consider only the matters the governor places before it. 📌 Review often pairs these: Who calls special sessions and decides the topics? → the governor (for both).
  • Function of the legislature: enacting bills and adopting resolutions (making public policy) — plus proposing constitutional amendments, adopting the budget, levying taxes, redistricting, impeaching/removing officials, and investigating.
  • Redistricting — occurs in the first odd-numbered year of a decade, based on the federal decennial census. Reapportionment = allotment of seats according to population; redistricting = drawing the new district lines to reflect that. If the legislature fails to redistrict (or the governor vetoes the plan), the Legislative Redistricting Board takes over — five ex officio members: lieutenant governor, Speaker, attorney general, comptroller, and land commissioner.
  • Gerrymandering — drawing districts to include or exclude certain voters so as to favor one group or party (districts often oddly shaped). Tactics: packing, cracking, and pairing/highjacking incumbents. The term comes from Massachusetts governor Elbridge Gerry (1812). Gerrymandered districts are legal as long as populations are equal and they don't dilute the vote based on race/color/language.
  • Single-member district — voters elect one member per district. Texas moved fully to single-member districts by 1975 (largely via court action). Compared with old multimember districts, single-member districts reduce campaign costs and elect more Black and Latino candidates.
  • Reynolds v. Sims (1964) established "one person, one vote" — legislative seats must be apportioned on a population basis.

LO 8.2 — Membership & Representation

  • Formal (constitutional) qualifications — House: age 21+, U.S. & Texas citizen, qualified voter, 2 years Texas residence, 1 year district residence. Senate: age 26+, 5 years Texas residence, 1 year district residence. A key legal restriction: a legislator may not hold another government office.
  • Informal qualifications (the "typical" legislator) — White, male, middle-aged, college-educated, a native Texan, and an attorney or businessperson who has served before. (Traditionally, the most common occupation is attorney.) Catholics are the most numerous religious group, followed by Baptists — but there is no religious qualification for office.
  • Descriptive representation — the lawmaker shares the characteristics of constituents (a woman representing women, a Latino representing Latinos). Substantive representation — the lawmaker works on behalf of a group's interests regardless of shared traits. Research finds increasing descriptive representation for a group improves substantive representation for that same group.
  • Compensation — salary is $600/month = $7,200/year, set in the constitution and unchanged since 1975 (can be changed only by amendment, which voters must approve). Legislators also receive a per diem (daily expense allowance — $221/day in 2021, set by the Ethics Commission) and a relatively generous pension after minimum service. Texas is one of 35 states with no term limits.

LO 8.3 — Presiding Officers, Committees & Caucuses

  • President of the Senate = the Lieutenant Governor. The lt. gov is not a senator (like the U.S. VP presiding over the U.S. Senate) but, unlike the VP, actively presides and leads on legislation. Chosen in a statewide election for a 4-year term; first in line to succeed the governor. Most of the lt. gov's power comes from Senate Rules, not the constitution — making the lt. gov arguably the most powerful officer in the state when the legislature is in session.
  • President pro tempore — a senator elected by the Senate who presides when the lieutenant governor is absent or disabled.
  • Speaker of the House — the presiding officer of the House, a representative elected by the House membership in an open (not secret) vote to a 2-year term. The Speaker controls all proceedings in the House and is often called the second most powerful official in the state. Powers of both presiding officers: appoint committee members and chairs, refer bills to committee, control floor action, break ties, and appoint conference-committee members.
  • Committee types:
    • Substantive committee — considers bills/resolutions on the subject named by the committee (e.g., Agriculture, Public Health).
    • Procedural committee — deals with internal legislative matters (e.g., the Calendars Committee, which sets when bills reach the floor).
    • Standing committee — permanent committees established under the rules each session.
    • Select committee — temporary; created to consider legislation crossing committee lines; may include non-legislators.
    • Interim / special interim committee — studies important policy issues between regular sessions.
    • Conference committee — reconciles differing House and Senate versions of the same bill; five members from each chamber (at least three from each must agree). 📌 Review: "What happens when the House and Senate pass a bill with different language?" → it goes to a conference committee to work out a compromise.
  • Caucus — a group of legislators organized around party, ideology, race/ethnicity, or a specific issue. Caucuses have no formal legal role but must register with the Texas Ethics Commission. The Legislative Black Caucus is an example of a racial/ethnic caucus; there are also party caucuses, ideological caucuses (Texas Freedom Caucus), and bipartisan issue caucuses.

LO 8.4 — Bills, Resolutions, Oversight, Impeachment & Veto

  • Bill — a proposed law before enactment (House Bill / Senate Bill). Three categories: special bill (an exception to general law for a specific individual, class, or corporation), general bill (applies to all people/property in Texas), and local bill (creates or affects a single unit of local government — a city, county, or special district). To become law, a bill needs a simple majority in both chambers; a two-thirds vote is needed for an emergency measure to take effect immediately.
  • Resolutions (know the majorities and whether the governor signs):
    • Simple resolution — action by one chamber only; NOT sent to the governor (internal matters, e.g., adopting rules).
    • Concurrent resolution — passed by both chambers and sent to the governor (e.g., to adjourn, or to express the legislature's will).
    • Joint resolution — passed by both chambers; used to propose amendments to the Texas Constitution and to ratify amendments to the U.S. Constitution. Joint resolutions proposing amendments are NOT signed by the governor. 📌 Review: "When Texas ratifies an amendment to the U.S. Constitution, what legal form is used?" → a joint resolution.
  • How a bill becomes a law (House-origin path — at least four major steps): 1. Introduction — a member files the bill electronically with the chief clerk. 2. First reading & referral — the reading clerk reads the caption; the Speaker refers it to a committee. 3. Committee consideration & report — hearings, possible amendments; the bill can be "pigeon-holed" (killed by inaction). 4. Second reading, debate & vote (simple majority). 5. Third reading & final passage. Then the Senate repeats the readings/committee/votes. If the versions differ, a conference committee reconciles them; then enrollment, signatures of the presiding officers, and action by the governor.
  • First reading in the House — the bill's caption is read and the bill is referred to a committee by the Speaker (no debate at first reading).
  • Oversight — the legislature's power to monitor state agencies, largely by requiring agencies to make periodic and special reports. Tools include the state auditor (appointed by the Legislative Audit Committee) and the Sunset Advisory Commission, which reviews nearly every agency every 12 years and recommends whether to continue, merge, or abolish it. Under senatorial courtesy, the Senate will reject an appointee deemed "personally objectionable" by the senator from the appointee's district.
  • Approving the governor's appointments — most gubernatorial board/commission appointments to head state agencies must be approved by at least two-thirds of the senators present.
  • Impeachment & removal — the House impeaches (brings charges by a simple majority of members present); the official is suspended; the Senate tries and convicts by a two-thirds majority of its membership, with the Chief Justice of the Texas Supreme Court presiding. Punishment is limited to removal and disqualification from office (Gov. James Ferguson was removed this way in 1917). This is the process to remove a governor from office.
  • Filibuster — a Senate delaying tactic: a senator speaks as long as physical endurance permits to block a vote (most effective near a deadline). Wendy Davis's 2013 abortion-bill filibuster is the famous example.
  • Chubbing — a House delaying tactic: members debate bills at length (often "buffer bills" they don't oppose) to run out the clock and prevent a vote on another bill. ⚠️ Note a textbook glitch: the current edition's Learning Check answer says chubbing is used "to hasten a vote" — that's a typo. Chubbing is a delay tactic (the main text calls it "this delaying action"), and the older edition's answer key correctly says "delay."
  • Ghost voting — pressing another representative's voting button. House rules prohibit it unless the absent member gave permission.
  • Veto & the governor's options. While the legislature is in session, the governor may: sign the bill; let it become law unsigned after 10 days (not counting Sundays); or veto it (which can be overridden by two-thirds of both chambers). After the legislature adjourns, the governor has 20 days (counting Sundays) to act; a bill not vetoed becomes law. A post-adjournment veto is especially powerful because the legislature is gone and cannot override it. 📌 Review: "What is the fate of a bill vetoed after the legislature has adjourned?" → it dies and cannot be overridden (post-adjournment veto). "How long does the governor have after adjournment to act?" → 20 days.
  • ⚠️ Pocket veto — on your review, but Texas has none. A federal pocket veto lets an executive kill a bill by taking no action while the legislature is adjourned so it can't override. In Texas the opposite is true: an unsigned bill becomes law (after 10 days in session, 20 days after adjournment). The closest Texas analog is the post-adjournment veto (an active veto after adjournment that can't be overridden). Sourced externally — not in any provided material; verified against Texas Government 2.0 / OERTX.

LO 8.5 — Influences on Legislators' Voting

  • In theory, constituents (especially voters) matter most, because they elect legislators. In practice, legislators are heavily influenced by the presiding officers, the governor (a threatened veto can kill a bill in committee), the attorney general and comptroller (who estimates revenue), and lobbyists.
  • Research organizations ("think tanks") & official bodies:
    • Texas Legislative Council (TLC) — nonpartisan; drafts bills, provides research, and runs the "Red Apple" redistricting software.
    • House Research Organization (HRO) — bipartisan, independent of leadership; produces the Daily Floor Report with arguments for and against each bill.
    • Senate Research Center — analyzes bills for the Senate.
    • Every Texan (formerly the Center for Public Policy Priorities) — focuses on low/moderate-income families; critics call it too liberal.
    • Texas Public Policy Foundation (TPPF) — conservative; described as the "in-house think tank of the state's current Republican leadership."
  • The media (Texas Tribune, Quorum Report, Austin American-Statesman) also shape legislators' decisions and priorities.
