Chapter 17 — Go West Young Man (Sec 17.1–17.4)
Westward Expansion, 1840–1900
▼
Obj 1–2: Great Plains & Manifest Destiny
The Great Plains were the last settled area because early explorers like Major Stephen Long called it a "Great American Desert" — hostile climate, few trees, and perceived as unfit for farming. By the 1840s, economic opportunity and ideology shifted American thinking.

Manifest Destiny was coined by magazine editor John O'Sullivan in 1845 — the belief that Americans were divinely ordained to expand democratic institutions across the continent, exposing Native peoples to Protestant values and "civilizing" development.

Manifest Destiny John O'Sullivan Great Plains
Obj 3a: Farming — Homestead Act
Congress passed the Homestead Act (1862) — any head of household or individual over 21 (including women and African Americans) could claim 160 acres of public land, live on it for 5 years, and own it. The Pacific Railway Act (1862) was passed the same year, enabling a transcontinental railroad.

Challenges: Harsh weather, droughts, insects (grasshopper plagues), isolation, and debt.
Innovations: Steel plow (broke tough sod), windmill (water access), dry farming techniques, and barbed wire (cheap fencing without trees).

Homestead Act 1862 Pacific Railway Act barbed wire steel plow
Obj 3b: Mining — Gold & Silver
Major strikes: Comstock Lode (Nevada, 1859) — massive silver deposit; Pike's Peak, Colorado (1859) — gold rush. Mining towns were rough boomtowns that grew explosively and often collapsed just as fast.

Comstock Lode Pike's Peak boomtown
Obj 3c: Ranching — Cowboys & Railroads
The cattle industry exploded after the Civil War. Cowboys drove herds north on the Chisholm Trail to railroad towns (Abilene, Dodge City). The myth: romantic, free, heroic cowboy. The reality: dangerous, low-paid, often minority (many cowboys were Black or Hispanic), seasonal labor.

Chisholm Trail Abilene open range
Obj 4: Plains Indians & the Dawes Act
Plains Indians (Sioux, Cheyenne, Comanche) were nomadic, centered on buffalo hunting. The U.S. government systematically destroyed the buffalo herds to undermine Native life. Conflicts: Sand Creek Massacre (1864), the Battle of Little Bighorn (1876) — Custer's Last Stand, and the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890).

The Dawes Severalty Act (1887): broke up tribal communal lands into individual 160-acre plots to force assimilation. "Surplus" land sold to white settlers. Native Americans not granted formal citizenship until 1924.

Chapter 18 — Industrialization (Sec 18.1–18.3)
The Gilded Age Economy
▼
Obj 5: Inventions of the Late 1800s
The U.S. Patent Office issued nearly 450,000 patents between 1860–1890.

Thomas Edison — incandescent light bulb (1879), phonograph, first commercial electric power station (NYC, 1882). Used direct current (DC).
George Westinghouse promoted alternating current (AC) which could travel farther.
Alexander Graham Bell — telephone (1876). By 1900 over 1.35 million telephones in use.
Bessemer & open-hearth processes — cheap mass steel. U.S. steel output grew from 13,000 tons (1860) to 10 million tons (1900).

Thomas Edison DC power George Westinghouse AC power Alexander Graham Bell Bessemer process telephone 1876
Obj 6: Railroads — America's First Big Business
Railroads were the first industry to require massive capital, complex management, and nationwide coordination. The government supported growth with land grants and loans. The transcontinental railroad was completed at Promontory Summit, Utah (1869) — Central Pacific (west) met Union Pacific (east).

Transcontinental RR 1869 land grants Promontory Summit
Obj 7: Business Leaders — Carnegie, Rockefeller, Morgan
Andrew Carnegie
Steel magnate. Classic rags-to-riches story. Used vertical integration — owned everything from iron ore mines to steel mills. Wrote The Gospel of Wealth. Sold Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for $480M.
John D. Rockefeller
Oil tycoon. Founded Standard Oil (1870). Used horizontal integration — bought out all competitors. Created the trust. Controlled 90% of U.S. oil refining at peak.
J.P. Morgan
Banker/financier. Bought Carnegie Steel and created U.S. Steel (1901) — first billion-dollar corporation. Represented the power of investment banking.

vertical integration horizontal integration trust Gospel of Wealth robber barons
Obj 8: Workers & Labor Conditions
Factory workers faced 12–14 hour days, 6–7 days/week, dangerous conditions, child labor, and no job security. No workers' compensation, no safety regulations. The factory system replaced skilled craftsmen with unskilled machine operators.
Obj 9: Labor Unions & Major Strikes
Terrence Powderly led the Knights of Labor (KOL) — open to all workers regardless of skill, race, or gender. Peak: 700,000 members.
Samuel Gompers founded the American Federation of Labor (AFL, 1886) — focused on skilled workers, bread-and-butter issues.

Key Strikes:
• Great Railroad Strike (1877) — first national strike; Hayes sent federal troops; ~100 killed.
• Haymarket Riot (1886, Chicago) — KOL-adjacent rally; bomb thrown; 8 anarchists convicted; devastated KOL.
• Homestead Steel Strike (1892) — Frick hired Pinkertons; violent clash; strike broken; union crushed.
• Pullman Strike (1894) — Eugene V. Debs led; Cleveland sent federal troops; Debs jailed; strike broken.

Knights of Labor AFL Terrence Powderly Samuel Gompers Haymarket 1886 Homestead 1892 Pullman 1894
Chapter 19 — City Life (Sec 19.1–19.3)
Urbanization, Immigration & Social Reform
▼
Obj 10: Urbanization & Challenges
U.S. cities exploded after the Civil War. Key innovations: electric streetcars, steel-frame skyscrapers, electric elevators, and indoor plumbing. Challenges: overcrowding, tenement slums, disease, poor sanitation, crime, fire hazards, and corrupt political machines (e.g., Tammany Hall).
Solutions: Settlement houses (Jane Addams' Hull House, Chicago, 1889), sanitation reforms.

Hull House Jane Addams tenements Tammany Hall
Obj 11: Great Migration & New Immigration
Great Migration: Large movement of African Americans from the rural South to northern industrial cities, driven by Jim Crow laws, racial violence, and the promise of jobs.

Immigration shifted from Northern/Western Europe (Germany, Ireland) to Southern/Eastern Europe (Italy, Poland, Russia). Nativism rose in response. Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) — first major immigration restriction law.

Great Migration nativism Chinese Exclusion Act 1882
Obj 12: Social Reform Movements
Social Gospel Movement: Protestant clergy argued Christian ethics demanded addressing social problems — poverty, child labor, inequality.
The "New Woman": Growing female independence — more women attending college, entering workforce, and pushing for suffrage.
Chapter 20 — Politics of the Gilded Age (Sec 20.1–20.3)
Corruption, Reform & the Populist Movement
▼
Obj 13: The "Gilded Age"
Mark Twain coined "The Gilded Age" in his 1873 novel: gilded = gold-covered on the outside but corrupt underneath. Era of massive wealth inequality and political corruption despite rapid economic growth.
Obj 14: Civil Service Reform
Spoils system: giving government jobs to political party loyalists regardless of qualifications. President Garfield was assassinated by a disappointed office-seeker in 1881. The Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883) created merit-based federal hiring through competitive exams.

Pendleton Act 1883 spoils system civil service reform
Obj 15: The "Weak" Presidents (1877–1896)
Rutherford B. Hayes · James Garfield (assassinated 1881) · Chester Arthur · Grover Cleveland (2 non-consecutive terms) · Benjamin Harrison. None asserted significant executive authority.
Obj 16–18: Populist Movement & the 1896 Election
Farmers' grievances: falling crop prices, high railroad rates, deflation (gold standard made debts harder to pay). Grange (1867) and Farmers' Alliance organized farmers.

People's Party (Populists, 1892) demanded: free coinage of silver, government ownership of railroads, graduated income tax, direct election of senators.

Election of 1896: William Jennings Bryan (Democrat/Populist) — "Cross of Gold" speech: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." William McKinley (Republican) — gold standard, industry. McKinley won.

Populist decline: Fusion with Democrats alienated urban workers who feared inflation. Gold discovered in Alaska/South Africa naturally inflated money supply. Party faded.

Populist Party 1892 free silver Cross of Gold 1896 McKinley Bryan

CH.17
Discuss the impact of westward expansion on Native American peoples. How did U.S. government policies like the Dawes Severalty Act of 1887 affect tribal life and sovereignty?
Check what you covered:
Westward expansion devastated Native American peoples through a combination of military violence, deliberate destruction of their food supply, and federal policy designed to eliminate tribal culture. The U.S. Army conducted a campaign of conquest marked by atrocities including the Sand Creek Massacre (1864), where Colorado militia killed ~150 Cheyenne and Arapaho, and the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890), where ~250 Lakota Sioux were killed. The Battle of Little Bighorn (1876) — Custer's Last Stand — was the last major Native military victory, but it prompted intensified U.S. military campaigns. The Dawes Severalty Act (1887) was perhaps the most damaging policy: it broke up tribal communal lands into individual 160-acre plots to force assimilation into white agrarian society. The "surplus" land left over was sold to white settlers, and tribes lost millions of acres. Children were sent to boarding schools like the Carlisle Indian Industrial School to strip away language, clothing, religion, and customs. Native Americans were not granted formal U.S. citizenship until 1924 — decades after the destruction of their way of life.
CH.18
Compare and contrast the business strategies of Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller. How did each use their respective integration strategies to build industrial empires, and what were the social consequences?
Check what you covered:
Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller both built industrial empires using different integration strategies. Carnegie dominated steel through vertical integration — he owned every stage of production, from iron ore mines and coal fields to railroads and steel mills to distribution channels. This allowed him to cut costs at every stage. His Carnegie Steel Company sold to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for $480 million, becoming U.S. Steel — the first billion-dollar corporation. Rockefeller dominated oil through horizontal integration — he systematically bought out every competitor in oil refining until Standard Oil controlled approximately 90% of U.S. oil refining. He also pioneered the "trust" — a legal structure where competing companies transferred their stock to a board of trustees, allowing centralized control while maintaining the appearance of separate companies. The social consequences of both strategies were profound: monopoly power eliminated competition and artificially controlled prices; workers in these industries faced dangerous conditions, 12–14 hour days, and poverty wages; the concentration of wealth in a few hands created the stark inequality that defined the Gilded Age. The Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) represented the first federal attempt to limit monopolistic practices, though it was largely unenforced at first.
CH.19
Describe the major challenges created by rapid urbanization in late 19th century America. What reform efforts emerged in response, and how successful were they?
Check what you covered:
Rapid urbanization in the late 19th century created severe social crises. American cities grew faster than infrastructure could accommodate, producing dangerous overcrowding, tenement slums, inadequate sanitation, rampant disease, crime, and fire hazards. Corrupt political machines like Tammany Hall in New York exploited these conditions, trading services like jobs and housing help for the votes of desperate immigrants and the poor. Reformers responded on multiple fronts. Jane Addams founded Hull House in Chicago in 1889, creating the model settlement house — a community center providing education, childcare, job training, and social services directly in poor neighborhoods. Muckraking journalists like Jacob Riis, whose How the Other Half Lives (1890) documented NYC tenement conditions with photographs, shocked middle-class audiences and built political will for reform. The Social Gospel movement mobilized Protestant churches to address poverty and inequality. The period also saw demographic transformation: the Great Migration brought hundreds of thousands of African Americans north from Jim Crow laws and racial violence, though they found discrimination in northern cities too. A new wave of immigrants arrived from Southern and Eastern Europe, fueling nativist backlash and eventually the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) — the first major law restricting immigration by nationality.
CH.20
Explain the rise and fall of the Populist Party. What were the major grievances of farmers, what solutions did Populists propose, and why did the party decline after the Election of 1896?
Check what you covered:
The Populist Party rose from the genuine economic distress of American farmers in the late 19th century. Falling crop prices, exploitative railroad freight rates, and a deflationary gold standard combined to trap farmers in cycles of debt — prices for their crops fell while their fixed debt obligations stayed the same, in real terms. The Grange (1867) and Farmers' Alliance organized farmers politically, eventually forming the People's (Populist) Party in 1892. The Omaha Platform was their bold agenda: free coinage of silver (to inflate the money supply and ease debt), government ownership of railroads and telegraph lines, a graduated income tax, and direct election of senators. In the crucial Election of 1896, Populists fused with the Democratic Party and supported William Jennings Bryan, who delivered the famous "Cross of Gold" speech: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." But Republican William McKinley, backed by business interests, won decisively. The coalition failed: urban workers in the North feared inflation that would raise their cost of living, and business interests had the money to outspend Bryan. After the election, new gold discoveries in Alaska and South Africa naturally increased the money supply, easing the deflation that farmers had complained about. Without their core issue, the Populist coalition dissolved, and the party declined rapidly after 1896.
