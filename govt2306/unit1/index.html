Chapter 1 — Key Terms
The Environment of Texas Politics — 17 Terms
▼
TGovernment
A public institution with authority (power) to allocate values through public policies (laws). Three branches: Legislative, Executive (governor), Judicial. Delegates lawmaking to local governments (counties, cities).
TPolitics
The process of making policies (laws). A product of political activity between those in power and those who influence them (voters). Involves conflict between parties/groups but requires cooperation. About who gets power, how they get it, and what they do with it.
TPublic Policy
Government action designed to meet a public need or goal as determined by elected or authorized officials. In Texas: enacted by legislature, approved by governor, implemented by executive departments, interpreted by courts.
TPolitical Culture
Attitudes, habits, and general behavior patterns that develop over time and affect the political life of a state and region. Gives each state its character. Texas has THREE cultures: moralistic, individualistic, and traditionalistic.
TMoralistic Culture
Originated with New England Puritanism. Views political participation as a civic duty. Government = public service expected to advance the public good. Least common culture in Texas.
TIndividualistic Culture
Influenced by the frontier experience. Sense of rugged individualism and limited government. Pro-business, anti-regulation. Government should only maintain order. Daniel Elazar identifies Texas as strongly individualistic.
TTraditionalistic Culture
Product of the Old South plantation/patron system. Government maintains the status quo for a few elite citizens. Historically excluded poor and minorities. Influenced by Jim Crow laws, elitism, racism, conservatism. Features: favorable business climate, right to work, eminent domain.
TFrontier Experience
19th-century frontier life that shaped Texas's individualistic and traditionalistic political cultures. Features: use of force to settle disputes, rugged self-reliance, social conservatism, distrust of government, pro-business, low voter participation.
TRailroad Commission of Texas
State agency with regulatory jurisdiction over the oil and gas industry. Originally created to regulate railroads. One of the oldest and most powerful regulatory agencies in Texas. Four of the ten largest U.S. oil fields are in Texas.
TTexas Water Development Board
State agency for water planning and management. Key 21st-century challenge: demand for water will rise 22% by 2060. Water use per fracking well increased 770%. Texas minimally regulates industry and agriculture on water.
TUrbanization
Migration from rural to urban areas. Texas was 80% rural in 1900; today approximately 85% urban. Government policies historically enforced residential segregation: redlining, urban renewal, exclusionary zoning, racial covenants.
TUrban Renewal
Government policy of taking private property for public projects. Historically used as a discriminatory tool to enforce residential segregation, often displacing minority communities.
TMetropolitization
Concentration of people in urban centers that become linked as a single geographic entity. Types: Micropolitan (10,000-50,000), Metropolitan (50,000+), Metropolitan Division (2.5M+). Texas had 5 of the fastest-growing metro areas in the U.S.
TEminent Domain
Government's power to take private property for public use with compensation. A reserved state power. Also a feature of traditionalistic culture in Texas - historically used against minority and lower-income communities.
TGentrification
Process where higher-income residents move into urban neighborhoods, displacing lower-income residents and driving up property prices and ad valorem taxes.
TProgressive
Political ideology favoring active government, reform, and expanded public services to address social and economic inequality. Contrasts with the conservative/traditionalistic culture dominant in Texas.
TRedlining
A discriminatory rating system for loan risks used to deny mortgages to residents of minority neighborhoods. A key tool historically used to enforce residential segregation in Texas cities.

QAmong the 50 states, where does Texas currently rank in population?
Texas ranks 2nd in population (behind California), with 29+ million residents. Texas is increasingly diverse.
QAccording to Professor Daniel Elazar, the political culture of Texas is _______________.
Individualistic (with strong traditionalistic elements). Elazar asserts Texas wants little government intervention and only to maintain order. Texas's political culture developed under the flags of six countries. It is the only state that was its own independent republic (with president, congress, military, currency).
QOf the 50 states, where does Texas rank in land area?
Texas is the 2nd largest state, behind Alaska, with more than 267,000 square miles.
QIn what geographic area is Houston situated?
Houston is situated in the Gulf Coastal Plains region of Texas (Harris County - one of the four most populous counties).
QThe only region of Texas classified as mountainous is _______________.
The Basin and Range Province (Trans-Pecos/West Texas). Contains the Guadalupe Mountains and Big Bend area.
QWhat is the origin of Texas cattle ranching?
Texas cattle ranching traces its origin to cattle transported from Spain to Mexico to Texas, resulting in the Texas longhorn. Texas still leads the nation in cattle production.
QWhat four land-based industries have been influential in the development of Texas?
1. Cattle - from Spanish/Mexican origins; Texas still leads the nation
2. Cotton - backbone of economy 1900s; Texas yields ~50% of U.S. supply, ~10% of world's
3. Timber - Gulf Coastal plains; bonanza era mid-1800s through 1920s, then declined
4. Oil - Spindletop Field first major wells; Railroad Commission regulates; 40% of U.S. oil production
QApproximately what percent of Texans currently live in metropolitan areas?
Approximately 85% of Texans live in metropolitan areas (up from 80% rural in 1900).
QWhich group of the population in Texas is growing most rapidly?
Latinos (Hispanic Texans) - approximately 39% of the population and the fastest-growing group. Anglo population has fallen below 50%.
QDoes the service industry in Texas pay higher or lower wages?
The service industry pays lower wages than manufacturing. Most service jobs lack benefits. Services is the fastest-growing economic sector but comparatively low-paying.
QWhat are some of Texas's Social and Economic Challenges?
- Poverty: 1 in 4 children; 18.5% below poverty line; Texas near bottom in government response
- Healthcare: highest uninsured %; refused Medicaid expansion
- Education: ranks 38th in graduation rate; teacher shortage; 10%+ quit annually
- Water: demand up 22% by 2060; fracking water use up 770%
- Environment: leads nation in many types of pollution
- Immigration/Diversity: undocumented immigrants cost local govts ~$1.44 billion
Chapter 2 — Key Terms
Federalism and the Texas Constitution — 14 Terms
▼
TFederalism
Division of power between national and regional (state) governments. The heart of the system is the relationship between the two. Sharing powers creates tension. The balance of power constantly evolves.
TTenth Amendment
States that "powers not specifically given to the National government by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." The constitutional basis for state reserved powers. Texas has filed 60+ lawsuits against the federal government asserting 10th Amendment rights since 2008.
TNational Supremacy Clause
Found in Article VI of the U.S. Constitution. States that the Constitution and laws made under its authority are the supreme law of the land. All state officials are bound by it. When state and federal law conflict, federal law prevails.
TDistribution of Powers
Determined by listing the powers of the national government in the U.S. Constitution AND adding the Tenth Amendment. Three types: delegated (Art. I Sec. 8), implied (necessary and proper clause), and reserved (10th Amendment/states).
TDelegated and Implied Powers
Delegated (enumerated/expressed) powers: Article I, Section 8 - specifically listed (coin money, declare war, regulate interstate commerce).
Implied powers: From the Necessary and Proper (elastic) Clause - allows Congress to expand authority beyond what is specifically written.
TLimitations on the States
Article I, Section 10: States may NOT enter foreign treaties; coin money; secede (Texas v. White). Amendments: Cannot deny voting rights; cannot deprive citizens of life, liberty, or property without due process (14th Amendment); cannot deny equal protection.
TGuarantees to the States
U.S. Constitution guarantees states: Protection from invasion/domestic violence; cannot be combined or divided without consent; 2 senators per state; at least 1 House member; electoral votes = senators + representatives; equal participation in amendment process.
TInterstate Relations and State Immunity
Article IV and 11th Amendment: Privileges and Immunities Clause - citizens entitled to same rights in all states; Full Faith and Credit - civil actions of one state recognized in all states; 11th Amendment (sovereign immunity) - states generally cannot be sued in federal court.
TPrivileges and Immunities
Article IV: Citizens of each state are entitled to the same privileges and immunities in all other states - protection of life, liberty, and property. Prevents states from discriminating against citizens from other states.
TFull Faith and Credit
Article IV: Civil government actions and legal documents of one state must be recognized in all other states. Does NOT apply to criminal cases (handled through extradition). Example: marriage license, court judgment from one state valid in another.
TGrants-in-Aid
Federal money given to states to influence state policymaking. During the 20th century, the primary tool the federal government used to extend jurisdiction into areas traditionally belonging to state and local governments. States must follow conditions to receive funds.
TDevolution
Reduction in size of the federal government and shifting of expenditures and responsibilities to the states. Trend since the 1980s (Reagan). Gives states more freedom to make decisions. Texas generally supports devolution and resists national control.
TBlock Grants
Fixed sums of money awarded to states on an automatic formula. Give states MORE flexibility in spending compared to categorical grants. A key feature of the devolution trend - shifting power back to states.
TTexas Constitution of 1876
Texas's current (7th) constitution. Drafted by 75 Democrats, 15 Republicans; 40%+ Grange members. Slogan: 'retrenchment and reform.' Dismantled the Reconstruction (Davis) administration. Restricts all three branches. Creates a weak governor. ~92,000 words; amended 500+ times.
Chapter 2 — Review Questions Answered
Prof. Chapa's Official Exam Questions with Complete Answers
▼
QWhat are the two sources for the powers that govern Texas government?
1. The U.S. Constitution - delegates powers to the national government; sets guarantees and limitations on states
2. The Texas Constitution of 1876 - the primary source of the state government's policymaking power; defines structure of Texas government; authorizes creation of cities and counties
QThe 'necessary and proper' clause (Article I, Section 8) is an example of which type of power?
Implied powers. Also called the elastic clause. Allows Congress to expand its authority beyond what is specifically enumerated. How the federal government has significantly extended its reach.
QThe regulation of commerce among the several states by Congress is an example of what type of power?
Delegated (enumerated/expressed) power. Specifically listed in Article I, Section 8. The Commerce Clause has been one of the most powerful tools for expanding federal authority.
QCongress has expanded the scope of the national government through its grant of what type of power?
Implied powers (elastic/necessary and proper clause) and the Commerce Clause. During the 20th century, the federal government also used grants-in-aid to extend jurisdiction into areas traditionally within state/local government.
QUnder what terms is Texas allowed to divide into as many as five states?
Under the Joint Resolution of Annexation (1845), Texas retained the right to divide into as many as five states, with the consent of the Texas legislature and Congress. A unique condition of Texas statehood tied to its history as an independent republic.
QWhat are the constitutional guarantees to the States?
- Protection from invasion and domestic violence
- Cannot be combined or divided without their consent
- Equal Senate representation (2 senators per state)
- At least one House of Representatives member
- Electoral votes proportional to congressional representation
- Equal participation in the constitutional amendment process
- Crimes committed in a state tried in that state's courts
QWhat are the constitutional limitations on the States?
Article I, Section 10: Cannot enter foreign treaties; cannot secede (Texas v. White); cannot coin money.
Constitutional amendments: Cannot deny voting rights; cannot deprive citizens of life, liberty, or property without due process (14th Amendment); cannot deny equal protection of the laws.
QWhat part of the U.S. Constitution provides that citizens are entitled to the same privileges and immunities in other states?
Article IV - the Privileges and Immunities Clause. Ensures citizens of each state are entitled to the same rights and protections in all other states.
QWhat are the reserved powers of the States?
Reserved powers are NOT defined in the U.S. Constitution - broad powers retained by states under the 10th Amendment:
- Police power - public order, safety, health, morals
- Taxing power
- Proprietary power - own and operate public property
- Eminent domain - take private property for public use with compensation
QWhat has the trend in federal-state relations since the 1980s been?
The trend has been toward devolution - reducing federal government size and shifting expenditures and responsibilities to states. Texas resists national control over state power. Texas also sometimes refuses federal grants (e.g., refused Medicaid expansion).
QIn which ways can the federal government influence state policymaking?
- Grants-in-aid: conditional federal money with strings attached
- Block grants: fixed formula funds with more state flexibility
- Mandates: requirements states must follow
- Commerce Clause: extending federal regulation
- Supremacy Clause: federal law overrides conflicting state law
- Court decisions: Supreme Court can strike down state laws
QDuring the 20th century, the federal government used ___ to extend its jurisdiction into areas traditionally within state/local government.
Grants-in-aid. By attaching conditions to federal money, the national government extended its reach into education, transportation, healthcare, environmental regulation, and other areas traditionally managed by states.
QAccording to the text, Texas has had ___ constitutions.
Texas has had seven (7) constitutions:
1. Constitution of Coahuila y Tejas (1827)
2. Constitution of the Republic of Texas (1836)
3. Texas Constitution (1845 - statehood)
4. Secession Constitution (1861)
5. Reconstruction Constitution (1866)
6. Texas Constitution (1869)
7. Texas Constitution of 1876 (current)
QWhen was Texas annexed by the United States?
Texas was annexed by the United States in 1845. Texas was an independent republic from 1836-1845 with its own president, congress, military, and currency. Under the Joint Resolution of Annexation, Texas retained the right to divide into up to five states.
QHow are amendments to the Texas Constitution proposed?
A joint resolution proposing an amendment must be adopted by a two-thirds vote in each chamber of the Texas legislature (both House and Senate). Then published twice in newspapers, posted in each county courthouse at least 30 days before the election.
QHow are proposed amendments to the state constitution ratified?
Voters must approve proposed amendments by a simple majority in a regular or special election. Constitutional amendment election turnout is typically less than 10%. The governor's only role is to proclaim the amendment. As of 2019: 491 approved, 182 defeated.
QSeparation of powers divides power among _______________.
Separation of powers divides power among the three branches of government: Legislative, Executive, and Judicial. In Texas, Article II establishes separation of powers; Article III = Legislative; Article IV = Executive; Article V = Judicial.

Three Political Cultures (Daniel Elazar's Framework)
Moralistic: New England Puritan roots. Political participation = civic duty. Government = public service for common good. Least prevalent in Texas.

Individualistic: Frontier experience. Limited government, rugged self-reliance, pro-business, anti-regulation. Elazar: Texas is STRONGLY INDIVIDUALISTIC - wants only to maintain order.

Traditionalistic: Old South/plantation/patron system. Government preserves social order for elite. Historically excluded poor and minorities. Jim Crow laws, racism, conservatism. Favorable business climate, right to work, eminent domain.

Elazar: TX = Individualistic moralistic individualistic traditionalistic frontier experience patron system
Geography - 4 Major Regions
Texas: 2nd largest state (267,000+ sq mi, behind Alaska). Four regions:
1. Texas Great Plains (West Texas) - agriculture, Republican, Protestant fundamentalism
2. Gulf Coastal Plains - Houston, petrochemicals, mixed political base
3. Interior Lowlands - Central/North Texas; Austin (state govt, universities, high-tech); Dallas-Fort Worth (Republican stronghold)
4. Basin and Range Province - Trans-Pecos; ONLY mountainous region in Texas

Basin and Range = ONLY mountains Houston = Gulf Coastal Plains 2nd largest state
Demographics
Texas: 2nd in population (29M+, behind California). 85% urban today (vs. 80% rural in 1900). Most populous counties: Harris, Dallas, Bexar, Tarrant (combined 40%+ of state population).
Latinos (~39%) = fastest-growing group. Anglos now below 50%. African Americans growing slowly. Asian Americans one of largest populations in nation.

Latinos = fastest growing 85% urban 2nd in population
Four Land-Based Industries
1. Cattle - from Spain to Mexico to Texas longhorn; still leads nation
2. Cotton - backbone of economy 1900s; Texas ~50% of U.S. supply, 10% of world's
3. Timber - Gulf Coastal plains; bonanza era mid-1800s to 1920s; declined from clear-cutting, oil, drought
4. Oil - Spindletop first major wells; Railroad Commission regulates; 40% of U.S. oil production

Modern economy: Energy, High Technology, Biotechnology, Services (fastest-growing but lowest wages), Agriculture (2nd in U.S.), Trade (USMCA replaced NAFTA 2020; Maquiladoras).

- Water: Texas Water Development Board; demand up 22% by 2060
- Environment: leads nation in many types of pollution; challenges federal EPA enforcement
- Immigration/Diversity: Texas DREAM Act; $1.44B local govt costs for undocumented immigrants
Chapter 2 — Federalism & the Texas Constitution
Powers, Interstate Relations, Texas Constitution of 1876
▼
Distribution of Powers
Delegated/Enumerated: Article I, Sec. 8 - specifically listed (coin money, declare war, regulate interstate commerce).
Implied: Necessary and Proper/Elastic Clause - Congress can make laws needed to carry out delegated powers.
Commerce Clause: Article I, Sec. 8 - major tool for expanding federal power into state areas.
Reserved: 10th Amendment - all powers not delegated belong to states or people (police, taxing, proprietary, eminent domain).

delegated = Art I Sec 8 implied = elastic clause Commerce Clause reserved = 10th Amend
Guarantees & Limitations on States
Guarantees: Protection from invasion; cannot be combined/divided without consent; equal Senate representation; electoral votes; equal participation in amendments.

Limitations (Art. I, Sec. 10 + amendments): Cannot enter foreign treaties; cannot secede (Texas v. White); cannot coin money; cannot deny voting rights; cannot deny due process (14th Amendment).

Art I Sec 10 Texas v. White = no secession 14th Amendment
Interstate Relations
Privileges and Immunities (Art. IV): Citizens entitled to same rights in all states.
Full Faith and Credit (Art. IV): Civil government actions of one state recognized in all states. Criminal cases handled through extradition.
11th Amendment: Sovereign immunity - states generally cannot be sued in federal court.
Federal-State Relations & Evolution
Balance of power constantly evolves. Since 1980s: devolution - shifting responsibilities to states.
Federal tools: grants-in-aid (conditional money), block grants (fixed formula, more state flexibility), mandates, Commerce Clause, Supremacy Clause.
Texas: 60+ lawsuits against federal govt since 2008 asserting 10th Amendment rights. Refused Medicaid expansion.

Texas's 7th and current constitution. Reaction to Reconstruction.
Drafted by 75 Democrats, 15 Republicans; 40%+ Texas Grange members. Slogan: "retrenchment and reform."
Creates a weak governor - one of weakest in nation. Restricts all three branches.
~92,000 words; amended 500+ times (491 approved, 182 defeated as of 2019). Less than 10% voter turnout in amendment elections.
Amendment: 2/3 each legislative chamber → simple majority of voters → governor proclaims.
