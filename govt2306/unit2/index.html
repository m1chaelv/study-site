LO 3.1 Relationships between local government and all other governments ▼

Explain the relationships that exist between a local government and all other governments, including national, state, and other local governments.

Key concepts: Dillon's Rule vs. Cooley Doctrine, intergovernmental relations, grassroots challenges, unfunded mandates. Texas strictly follows Dillon's Rule — local governments have only the powers granted, implied, or indispensable under state law. Local governments are created by the state and must obey both state and national laws. About 88% of Texans live in metropolitan areas (current edition, LO 3.6).

LO 3.2 Forms of municipal government organization ▼

Describe the four forms of municipal government in Texas and the two legal classifications of cities.

Classifications: General-law cities (population 201+) vs. Home-rule cities (5,000+). Four forms: Strong mayor-council (Houston, Pasadena — most responsive), Weak mayor-council, Council-manager (most common in Texas home-rule cities), Commission. Home-rule cities may also use recall, initiative, and referendum.

LO 3.3 Rules and social issues that shape local government outcomes ▼

Identify the election rules and social issues that shape who wins and what policies get made at the local level.

Key concepts: Nonpartisan elections (no party labels), at-large vs. single-member district elections, cumulative voting (used to increase minority representation), redistricting (required under federal law), municipal services (police, fire, water, streets), revenue sources (property tax + sales tax are the two largest), bonds, fees, TIRZs (Tax Increment Reinvestment Zones).

LO 3.4 Structure and responsibilities of counties ▼

Analyze the structure and responsibilities of Texas's 254 counties.

Key facts: All counties have the same constitutional structure. Commissioners court (county judge + 4 commissioners) is the major policymaking body. County judge has both administrative AND judicial responsibilities. County sheriff keeps the peace. Other offices: county clerk, county tax assessor-collector, county auditor, county attorney, district attorney, justices of the peace. Revenue = primarily property taxes. Challenges: border counties, colonias, USMCA/NAFTA, lack of home-rule authority.

LO 3.5 Functions and importance of special districts ▼

Explain the functions of special districts and their importance to the community.

Two categories: (1) School districts (~1,000+ ISDs — most visible/important) — governed by elected boards of trustees, funded primarily by local property taxes. (2) Noneducation special districts (~2,300+) — water, sewer, fire, parks, housing, transit. Also: 50 community college districts funded by property taxes, state, and tuition. T. Boone Pickens water district case illustrates private abuse of public district authority.

LO 3.6 How local governments deal with metropolitan issues ▼

Discuss the ways that local governments deal with metropolitan-wide and regional issues.

Key concepts: Metro government (full consolidation — Louisville, KY example), Extraterritorial jurisdiction (ETJ — limited authority outside city limits; ETJ abolished in 2019 by Texas legislature), Annexation (now requires vote or petition of those to be annexed), Councils of Government (COGs — 24 regional planning bodies). 88%+ of Texans live in metro areas.

LO 4.1 Role of political parties in Texas ▼

Evaluate the role of political parties in Texas.

Key concepts: A political party is chiefly concerned with recruitment, nomination, and election of citizens to governmental office. Texas uses a two-party system (Democratic and Republican). Single Member District (SMD) plurality system favors two parties. Political parties are NOT mentioned in the U.S. Constitution. Texas Election Code mandates both major parties have the same structure.

LO 4.2 Political ideologies in Texas ▼

Compare and contrast the different political ideologies found in the Lone Star State.

Conservative: Prefer minimal government in social/economic matters; split into fiscal conservatives (reduced taxes/spending) and social conservatives (family values, oppose abortion/gay marriage). Liberal: Favor government regulation of economy and aid to unemployed/poor; limited government in personal/religious matters. Neoliberal: Less government regulation of business but more government involvement in social programs (common among Texas Democrats). Libertarian: Minimal government in BOTH economic AND social matters. Because of Texas's individualistic and traditionalist culture, both Democrats and Republicans tend to be conservative.

LO 4.3 Electoral trends in Texas ▼

Identify electoral trends in Texas, including realignments, third parties, and independent candidates.

Realignment: Voters shift from one party to another (Texas Democrats → Republicans in 1990s). Dealignment: Voters no longer identify with any party. Third parties: Measured by ability to raise awareness, not win elections. Populist Party (1890s — agriculture), La Raza Unida (1970s — Latino rights), Libertarian Party, Green Party. Duverger's Law: SMD plurality systems favor two parties; proportional representation favors multiparty. Independents: Must collect signatures equal to 1% of total governor's race vote. Straight-ticket voting eliminated after 2018.

LO 4.4 Evolution of political parties in Texas ▼

Trace the evolution of political parties in Texas from the 1840s to the present.

1840s–1870s: Dominated by Democrats (Pro-Houston vs. Anti-Houston factions). Reconstruction (1865–1873): Republicans controlled Texas (E.J. Davis). 1870s–1970s: Solid Democratic one-party state. 1970s–1990s: Emerging two-party competition; Bill Clements first Republican governor since Reconstruction (1978); Kay Bailey Hutchison first woman senator (1993). 2000s+: Republican dominance; no Democrat has won statewide office since 1998. Future: Younger, more diverse voters trending Democratic; Great Recession era shaped younger generation away from Republicans.

LO 4.5 Political party system in Texas ▼

Describe the political party system in Texas — structure, conventions, and delegate selection.

Four levels: National, state, county, precinct. Parties organized as stratarchies (power diffused across levels). Temporary organization: Primaries + conventions (precinct → county/district → state). State convention adopts platform, elects delegates, selects presidential electors. Permanent organization: Precinct chairs, county executive committees, State Executive Committee (highest — one man, one woman from each of 31 senatorial districts). State chair is chief fundraiser. General election: Winner = most votes (plurality) — no runoff. Texas has 40 electoral votes.

📍 Chapter 3: Local Governments▼

LO 3.1 — Local Government & Federalism

  • Dillon's Rule — Local govts have ONLY powers granted by the state, implied by the grant, or indispensable to functioning. Texas follows this.
  • Cooley Doctrine — Local self-governance is an absolute right the state cannot take away. Followed in ~10 states, NOT Texas.
  • Texas local governments are created by the state, subject to the state, and must obey both state and national laws.
  • Intergovernmental relations — The relationship between and among different types of governments (national, state, local).
  • Grassroots challenges: 85%+ of Texans live in cities; voter apathy (fewer than 10% vote in local elections); funding pressures; infrastructure; demographic diversity.
  • Local govt has the greatest effect on daily lives (not national or state).

LO 3.2 — Municipal Government Forms

  • General-law city — Population 201+; follows state-prescribed charter; less flexibility.
  • Home-rule city — Population 5,000+; drafts its own charter by majority vote; more flexibility. Charter sets officer powers, salaries, terms, procedures.
  • Principal advantage of home-rule — Flexibility in organization and operation.
  • 3 extra powers of home-rule cities: Recall (remove officials), Initiative (propose law by petition + popular vote), Referendum (approve or repeal existing law by petition).
  • Strong mayor-council — Mayor appoints/removes department heads, has veto power. Most responsive to citizens. Popular among large cities. Only Houston and Pasadena in Texas.
  • Weak mayor-council — Mayor has limited power; shares authority with council.
  • Council-manager — Most common in Texas home-rule cities. Mayor elected at-large with few formal powers; council appoints a professional city manager to run departments. Criticized for being more responsive to elites than everyday citizens.
  • Commission — Elected commissioners form a policymaking board; each heads a department. Lacks a single executive. Not used in pure form by any Texas city today.
  • Minimum population to become a city in Texas: 201.

LO 3.3 — Municipal Politics, Elections & Revenue

  • Nonpartisan elections — All Texas city and special district elections; candidates listed without party labels. Effect: lower turnout, reduced minority representation, more personal/less issue-oriented.
  • At-large elections — Elected citywide; tend to overrepresent White middle class.
  • Single-member district elections — Elected by neighborhood; increases minority representation.
  • Cumulative voting — When multiple seats vacant, voters cast one or more votes in any combination. Used in ~50 Texas local governments to increase minority representation.
  • Redistricting — Required by federal law; city council district lines redrawn to represent equal population. Challenged due to gerrymandering. Most impacts Latino Texans.
  • Municipal services: Police, fire, water, streets, sewer, sanitation, parks.
  • Two largest tax sources: Property taxes + Sales taxes.
  • Other revenue: Fees, bonds, certificates of obligation, TIRZs (Tax Increment Reinvestment Zones).
  • When revenue falls short → cities cut services (especially those serving politically weaker citizens).
  • City charter — Legal document establishing powers of city government.
  • Ordinance — Laws passed by a city (equivalent of state statutes at local level).

LO 3.4 — County Government

  • Texas has 254 counties — more than any other state.
  • All counties have the same basic constitutional structure (Texas Constitution mandates it).
  • Counties are considered administrative arms of the state under the Texas Constitution.
  • Commissioners court — Major policymaking body; 4 elected commissioners (by precinct) + county judge. Approves budget.
  • County judge — Most prominent; has BOTH administrative AND judicial responsibilities.
  • County sheriff — Responsible for keeping the peace; chief law enforcement officer.
  • County clerk — Issues/maintains marriage licenses, vital records.
  • County tax assessor-collector — Collects property taxes.
  • County auditor — Financial oversight.
  • Primary revenue source: Property taxes.
  • Largest expenditures: Hospitals/health care, public safety, roads.
  • All elected county officials serve four-year terms (partisan elections).
  • Border counties — Challenges: colonias (2,300+ depressed settlements often without water/sewage), NAFTA/USMCA, undocumented immigration, border security, poverty.
  • Home-rule reform — Suggested but rejected; would give counties more ability to organize per local needs.

LO 3.5 — Special Districts

  • Texas has nearly 3,900 special districts — fastest growing form of local government.
  • Two categories: (1) School districts, (2) Noneducation special districts.
  • School districts (~1,000+ ISDs) — Most visible special district. Governed by popularly elected, nonsalaried boards of trustees. Funded primarily by local property taxes. Two main finance issues: equity and how much to spend.
  • Community college districts (50) — Different from public universities: receive local property taxes (public universities do not).
  • Noneducation special districts (~2,300+) — Water, sewer, fire, parks, housing. Exist because local needs cut across city/county lines.
  • Low public awareness = vulnerability to fraud/manipulation (T. Boone Pickens water district example).

LO 3.6 — Metropolitan Issues

  • 88%+ of Texans live in metro areas.
  • Metro government — Consolidation of local governments into one umbrella government. Example: Louisville-Jefferson County, Kentucky. No significant movement toward this in Texas.
  • ETJ (Extraterritorial jurisdiction) — Limited authority a city has outside its boundaries. Abolished in 2019 by the Texas Legislature.
  • Annexation — Making an outlying area part of a city. Now requires vote or petition of those to be annexed (changed in 2019).
  • COGs (Councils of Government) — 24 regional planning bodies created in the 1960s. Coordinate regional planning and implement state/federal programs.
  • The Starbase/SpaceX case (Boca Chica) illustrates the municipal incorporation process under Texas law.
🏛️ Chapter 4: Political Parties▼

LO 4.1 — Role of Political Parties

  • Political party — Organization chiefly concerned with recruitment, nomination, and election of citizens to governmental office.
  • Political parties are NOT mentioned in the U.S. Constitution.
  • Texas uses a two-party system (Democratic + Republican).
  • SMD plurality system — Winner = most votes (plurality). Favors two parties (Duverger's Law).
  • Proportional Representation (PR) — Seats allocated by percentage of vote. Favors multiparty systems (common in Europe).
  • Third party / minor party — Any party other than Democratic or Republican.
  • Texas Election Code mandates both major parties have the SAME structure.
  • General election — Held in November of even-numbered years; winner = plurality, no runoff needed.
  • Texas has 40 electoral votes.

LO 4.2 — Political Ideologies

  • Conservative — Minimal government in social and economic matters; split into fiscal conservatives (tax cuts, less spending) and social conservatives (family values, oppose abortion/gay marriage).
  • Liberal — Government regulation of economy; aid unemployed/poor; limited government in personal/religious matters.
  • Neoliberal — Less government regulation of business, BUT more government involvement in social programs. Common among Texas Democrats.
  • Libertarian — Minimal government in BOTH economic AND social areas.
  • Because of Texas's individualistic and traditionalistic culture, both Democrats and Republicans tend to be conservative.
  • The Tea Party (2009) made the Republican Party more conservative — focused on debt reduction, less spending, tax cuts.
⚠️ Neoconservatism appears on the test review but is NOT defined in your current or previous edition textbook. The PPT raises it as a learning check question but provides no answer slide. Standard definition: a conservative ideology that advocates active government intervention in foreign policy while maintaining conservative domestic positions. Use this definition but know it comes from outside the textbook.

LO 4.3 — Electoral Trends

  • Realignment — Voters shift allegiance from one party to another (e.g., conservative Texas Democrats → Republicans in 1990s).
  • Dealignment — Voters no longer identify with ANY political party.
  • Straight-ticket voting — Voting for all candidates of one party. Eliminated in Texas after 2018 election (law passed in 2017 session).
  • Duverger's Law — SMD plurality systems favor two-party systems; PR systems favor multiparty systems.
  • Third parties: Populist (1890s, agriculture), La Raza Unida (1970s, Latino rights, South Texas), Libertarian Party, Green Party. Success measured by issue awareness, not elections won.
  • Independent candidates — No party affiliation; must collect signatures equal to 1% of total vote in most recent governor's race for statewide ballot access.
  • Younger Texans (born 1979 and later) more likely to identify as Democrats — shaped by Iraq War, Great Recession, 2008 Obama election, Trump era.
  • Texas politics today is more partisan (party centered) than in earlier years.

LO 4.4 — History of Texas Political Parties

  • 1840s–1870s: Democrats dominated. Pro-Houston (Jackson Democrats) vs. Anti-Houston (Calhoun Democrats) factions.
  • Reconstruction (1865–1873): Republicans controlled Texas. E.J. Davis was Reconstruction governor.
  • 1870s–1970s: Solid one-party Democratic state. Fiercest political fighting was between conservative Democrats vs. liberal Democrats (between 1900–1950).
  • 1970s–1990s: Emerging two-party competition. Bill Clements — first Republican governor since Reconstruction (1978). Kay Bailey Hutchison — first Texas woman in U.S. Senate (1993).
  • 2000s–present: Republican dominance. No Democrat has won statewide since 1998. Republicans hold governorship throughout the 21st century.
  • Latinos and African Americans have strengthened the Democratic Party, especially in urban counties.
  • Principle difficulty for women candidates — Raising money; tend to face more scrutiny and access to campaign funds than male candidates.

LO 4.5 — Party Structure

  • Parties exist at 4 levels: National, state, county, precinct.
  • Organized as a stratarchy — power diffused among and within levels; state and local levels are free to decide their own positions.
  • Temporary organization — Primaries + conventions. Functions briefly to nominate candidates, pass resolutions, adopt platform, select delegates. Precinct → County/District → State.
  • Platform — Document setting forth the party's position on issues.
  • Precinct convention — Held every even-numbered year; usually sparsely attended; elects delegates to county convention.
  • State convention — Held in June of even-numbered years; writes party rules, adopts platform, in presidential years nominates delegates to national convention and elects presidential electors.
  • Permanent organization — Ongoing. Precinct chairs → County executive committees → State executive committee.
  • State Executive Committee — Highest permanent party body in state. One man, one woman from each of 31 senatorial districts. Elected by delegates at state convention. YES, Texas mandates equal men/women membership.
  • State party chair — Chief fundraiser. Elected by state convention delegates.
  • Why both parties have same structure — Texas Election Code mandates it.
  • PAC (Political Action Committee) — Raises/spends money to influence elections. Monitored by Texas Ethics Commission.
  • Superdelegate — Democratic party official who can support any candidate at national convention regardless of primary results.
  • Why parties have primaries — To select candidates for the general election and local party officers.
