Group 1 · Data, Information & Computing History
LO 1Explain the importance and differences of data and information▼

Separate datum, data, and information, and show how processing turns one into the next.

  • Data are the fuel of computing — data are the reason for computing, networking, automation, and all digital processing; we constantly take in data through our senses.
  • Datum vs data — a datum is a single value, attribute, characteristic, or identity of something (Merriam-Webster: “something given…as a basis for reasoning”); data is the plural of datum, so two datums are data.
  • Information — the result or product of data being processed; it has context relevant to an inquiry. “Information without context is just data.”
  • Context gives meaning — “blue” and “sky” are each a datum with little meaning alone; combined as “blue sky” they gain context and can answer “What color is the sky?”
  • IPO model — the most basic model for processing data into information is Input → Process → Output; even complex programs break down into IPO steps.
LO 2Discuss the history of computers and their uses▼

Trace the five computer generations and separate “computing” from “computers.”

  • Miniaturization trend — computers started large and shrank as technology advanced; what once filled a university gymnasium now fits in a pocket.
  • Five generations — First (1940–1956, vacuum tubes); Second (1956–1963, transistors); Third (1964–1971, integrated circuits + operating systems); Fourth (1971–2010, microprocessor, IBM PC, Apple Macintosh, GUI); Fifth (2010–present, artificial intelligence).
  • Computing vs computers — computing is a processing operation (like the IPO model); computers are the people, machines, and devices that perform the processing.
  • “Computers” as a job title — the term once described people who “computed” accounts into ledgers.
  • Birth of the modern computer — historians generally place its relevant birth around 1940, though earlier developments also contributed.
Group 2 · The Internet & the World Wide Web
LO 3Explain why there was a need for the internet▼

Connect the 1957 Sputnik launch to the Cold War need for a survivable communications network.

  • Sputnik trigger — in 1957 the USSR launched Sputnik (about 23 in. in diameter, ~190 lb), energizing the technology boom and putting space, satellites, and science on the public’s mind.
  • Cold War fear — if the Soviets could orbit a satellite, they might launch a missile that could take down U.S. telephone communications; what was needed was a system that could withstand an attack and stay operational.
  • Agencies formed — the U.S. government began research grants and formed agencies such as NASA and DARPA to investigate advanced technologies (rockets, weapons, computers).
  • Licklider’s “galactic network” — in 1962 MIT scientist J.C.R. Licklider proposed a network of interconnected computers that could communicate should the phone system be destroyed.
  • Survivability via packet switching — Paul Baran and Donald Davies independently devised transmitting “packets” across a switched network; Davies’s term “packet switching” stuck, and it was the key to a network that could not be taken down by a single site’s destruction.
LO 4Discuss the early developments of internetworking▼

Follow ARPANET through TCP/IP to the networks that became today’s internet.

  • ARPANET — designed by Licklider’s team in 1966 and ready for testing in October 1969; the first message “LOGIN” (UCLA → Stanford) failed after “L” and “O,” but it proved the Interface Message Processor (IMP), the early precursor to a router.
  • From internetwork to “internet” — as ARPAnet added nodes (going international in 1971), it became an internetwork — a network of networks interconnected for communication; “internetwork” was shortened to “internet.”
  • TCP/IP — Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn developed TCP (originally including IP’s functions) in the early 1970s; in the early 1980s TCP/IP replaced the Network Control Protocol (NCP) and made a worldwide network possible.
  • TCP/IP model layers — the four-layer reference model, top to bottom: Application, Transport, Internet (Network), and Link (Network Interface/Access).
  • Key protocols — TCP (connection-oriented, guarantees delivery), IP (addressing), HTTP (transfers webpage content), UDP (loss-tolerant, no guarantee), POP3/SMTP (email), and FTP (file transfer).
  • NSFnet and others — NSFnet (1986) grew and replaced the older, slower ARPAnet in the late 1980s; NSFnet shut down in 1995 and control was privatized, opening the network to commercial use.
LO 5Identify the key developments and developers of the web▼

Name the people and pieces that turned hypertext into the World Wide Web.

  • Hypertext pioneers — Vannevar Bush conceived “Memex” (“As We May Think,” 1945); Ted Nelson coined “hypertext” in 1965 (part of “Xanadu”); Doug Engelbart demonstrated NLS in the 1968 “Mother of All Demonstrations” and is credited as the “inventor” of hypertext (also the mouse and GUI); Brown University (Andries van Dam) built HES (1967) and FRES (1968).
  • Tim Berners-Lee & CERN — frustrated by fragmented information, Berners-Lee (an engineer at CERN) proposed “Mesh,” then with Robert Cailliau a formal “WorldWideWeb” (W3) project: a web of hypermedia documents viewed with a browser on a client/server architecture.
  • Three central web elements — HTML (a web markup language based on SGML), the Universal Document Identifier (UDI, foundation for the URL/URI), and HTTP (with four design principles: client/server, request, response, termination).
  • Free to everyone — CERN announced the web would be free to everyone, just as the University of Minnesota announced Gopher would start charging fees — spiking interest in the web.
  • Browsers — Nicola Pellow built the Line Mode Browser (libWWW), the first generally available browser; Mosaic (Marc Andreessen & Eric Bina, NCSA, 1993) was the first generally available browser to display text and graphics together; Andreessen then founded Netscape (Navigator, 1994), and Microsoft licensed Mosaic code to build Internet Explorer (1995).
  • Web phases — Web 1.0 (Static/Read-Only), Web 2.0 (Social/Read-Write — sharing, blogs, wikis, social networking), Web 3.0 (Semantic Web — one large database, blockchain, decentralization), Web 4.0 (Intelligent/pervasive/ubiquitous Web).
Group 3 · Clients, Servers, Virtualization & Cloud
LO 6Explain servers, clients, and network virtualization▼

Define the client/server relationship and how virtualization simulates hardware in software.

  • Client/server — like ordering in a diner: a client (a network host) requests a resource, and a server (specialized software on a centralized system) retrieves it and sends it back.
  • A server is software — it performs, manages, or controls special functions on a network; one physical computer can run multiple servers, and a server can also act as a client. Types include application, mail, proxy, virtual, and web servers.
  • Client types — thin clients have very few on-board resources and run apps on servers; thick (fat/rich) clients have all resources on-board; hybrid clients lean thick but rely on servers for centralized services.
  • Virtualization — a virtual object (machine, server, router) is the simulation of its functions through software; creating virtual devices via software is called virtualization.
  • Hypervisor — the key element of a virtual environment, also called the virtual machine manager (VMM); it is essentially the OS for the virtual environment. Type 1 runs “bare metal” directly on hardware; Type 2 runs on top of a host OS.
  • Network virtualization — virtualizing a network splits bandwidth into manageable channels assigned in real time; storage virtualization makes resources on multiple servers appear as one entity; server virtualization makes multiple servers appear as one.
LO 7Identify the service types and delivery modes of cloud computing▼

Separate cloud service delivery models (the “what”) from deployment models (the “who”).

  • Cloud computing — access to resources or processing located beyond the local firewall, provided by dedicated (typically virtualized) internet-based servers; it shifts local processing to remote providers over the internet.
  • Why “the cloud” — telephone diagrams used a cloud image for switching between central offices (POTS/PSTN); the cloud came to represent the randomized switching of the internet.
  • Delivery models (the “what”) — NIST defines three: SaaS (subscribers access applications on a CSP’s servers via the browser), PaaS (customized programming/testing/modeling environments), and IaaS (servers, storage, networking, and virtualization delivered over the internet, pay-per-use).
  • Other “aaS” offerings — AIaaS, BaaS (backup), CaaS (a “full meal” combining SaaS/PaaS/IaaS), DaaS (desktop), NaaS (network), UaaS (unified communications), and VaaS (video-conferencing).
  • Deployment models (the “who”) — NIST defines three: private (restricted access, in-house or via a CSP), public (a private cloud offered over a public network; membership is the constant), and hybrid (public deployment with private-cloud restrictions). A community cloud is a common variation.

🗄️ Group 1 · Data, Information & Computing History▼

Data and Information

  • Data — the fuel and reason for computing, networking, automation, and all digital processing; we take in data constantly through our senses.
  • Datum — a single value, attribute, characteristic, or identity of something (Merriam-Webster: “something given…as a basis for reasoning or inference”). “Data” is the plural of “datum,” so two datums are data.
  • Information — the result or product of data being processed; it carries context relevant to a question. Information without context is just data.
  • “Blue sky” example — “blue” and “sky” are each a datum with little meaning alone; together they gain context and can answer “What color is the sky?”

The IPO model

The most basic model for processing data into information is IPO — Input → Process → Output. An input source is fed into a processing element that produces information. Even extremely complicated programs break down into a series of IPO steps, and the input or output of one process often feeds another.

Why the internet was needed

  • Sputnik (1957) — the USSR’s satellite (~23 in., ~190 lb) energized the tech boom and led the U.S. to form agencies such as NASA and DARPA.
  • Cold War concern — a survivable communications system was needed in case a missile took down the U.S. telephone network.
  • Licklider (1962) — proposed a “galactic network” of interconnected computers.
  • Packet switching — Paul Baran and Donald Davies independently devised it; Davies’s term stuck. It let a network survive the destruction of any single site.

ARPANET and the growth of the network (Table 1-2)

The ARPANET project (designed 1966, tested Oct 1969) sent the first message “LOGIN” from UCLA toward Stanford — it failed after “L” and “O,” but proved the Interface Message Processor (IMP), the early precursor to a router. Legacy technologies developed for/because of ARPANET

  • Email (1971) — Ray Tomlinson; chose the @ sign to separate local from global addresses.
  • Telnet (1972) — bidirectional link to work at a remote device’s command prompt.
  • FTP (1973) — Abhay Bhushan; transfer of files and bulk data between nodes.
  • IS-IS (1980s) — Radia Perlman; she also developed Spanning Tree Protocol (STP).
  • DNS (1983) — Paul Mockapetris and John Klensin.
  • OSI Reference Model (1984) — issued by ISO and ITU; the suite failed but the model endured.
  • NSFnet (1986) — NSF’s competing network; replaced ARPAnet in the late 1980s and shut down in 1995, after which control was privatized for commercial use.

The TCP/IP model — four layers

  • Application — where a host/server interacts with the network (email, FTP, Telnet); identifies senders/receivers and requests content.
  • Transport — packages/unpackages messages; flow control, error control, segmenting/desegmenting.
  • Internet (Network) Layer — addressing, forwarding, and routing to physically move messages between hosts.
  • Link (Network Interface/Access) — packages a message for the physical medium; local physical-level addressing.

Primary TCP/IP protocols

  • TCP — Transport Layer; connection-oriented; guarantees delivery of message segments.
  • IP — defines network addressing; in use as IPv4 and IPv6.
  • HTTP — transfers webpage content host-to-host (does not define the content); HTTPS is the secure version; client/server.
  • UDP — does not guarantee delivery; used for loss-tolerant streams (“fire hose” vs. TCP’s verified transmission).
  • Email — POP3 receives incoming mail; SMTP sends and distributes mail.
  • FTP — transfers files, especially large files (increasingly embedded in browsers).

Hypertext and its pioneers

  • Vannevar Bush — conceived “Memex” in “As We May Think” (1945).
  • Ted Nelson — coined “hypertext” (1965), part of “Xanadu.”
  • Doug Engelbart — demonstrated NLS in the 1968 “Mother of All Demonstrations”; credited as “inventor” of hypertext; also invented the mouse, GUI, and bitmapped displays.
  • Brown University (Andries van Dam) — built HES (1967, first working hypertext system) and FRES (1968).

The World Wide Web

  • Tim Berners-Lee — CERN engineer; proposed “Mesh,” then (with Robert Cailliau) the “WorldWideWeb” (W3) project.
  • Three central elements — HTML (markup based on SGML), UDI (basis for URL/URI), and HTTP.
  • HTTP’s four design principles — client/server, request, response, termination.
  • Free web — CERN made the web free; Minnesota began charging for Gopher.
  • Browsers — Line Mode Browser / libWWW (Nicola Pellow, first generally available); Mosaic (Andreessen & Bina, NCSA, 1993, first to show text + graphics together).

Battle of the browsers (timeline)

  • 1994 Netscape Navigator · 1995 Internet Explorer; JavaScript (Netscape + Sun)
  • 1996 IE 3.0 with CSS · 1999 Navigator source released as Mozilla
  • 2002 Firefox · 2004 Safari · 2008 Chrome
  • 2015 Edge · 2017 Firefox 54 (WebVR) · 2021 Chrome Manifest extensions

Search, SSI, and CGI

  • Search engine — software that locates and categorizes searchable content and returns a search engine results page (SERP); it uses web crawlers (spiders) to scan content and follow links.
  • SEO — improves the chance of a first-page SERP listing via link building, title tags, content optimization, meta descriptions, and keyword research. (Fewer than ~6% of searchers reach page two.)
  • Early search tools — Archie (first; FTP files), Yahoo!, AltaVista (natural language), Lycos (categories/keywords), Ask Jeeves. Google handles ~99% of searches.
  • SSI — Server-Side Includes; an interpreted scripting language to merge files into a page (.shtml, .stm, .shtm).
  • CGI — Common Gateway Interface; rules/processes that let a page interact with servers (e.g., processing form data).

Web 3.0 and blockchain

  • Blockchain — a peer-to-peer, decentralized, distributed ledger with no third-party authority. Benefits: availability, immutability, security (cryptography), and transparency; no single point of failure.
🖥️ Group 3 · Clients, Servers, Virtualization & Cloud▼

Client/server computing

  • The relationship — like a diner: a client (network host) requests a resource; a server (specialized software on a centralized system) retrieves it and returns it.
  • A server is software — it performs, manages, or controls special functions; one physical computer can run several servers, and a server can also be a client.
Server typeRole
ApplicationProvides processing services to clients (often thin clients); a “middleware” link to resources such as a database.
MailCollects, forwards, and manages email (e.g., Microsoft Exchange, Enstar Mailtraq).
ProxySits between clients and other servers to filter requests, cache content, and optimize communications.
VirtualSoftware-based emulation of a physical computer; today there are more virtual servers than physical ones.
WebEssentially file servers that provide content in response to browser (client) requests.

Types of clients

ClientDescription
ThinVery few on-board resources; runs applications on servers; minimal RAM/storage.
Thick (fat/rich)Has all hardware and resources on-board to perform nearly all functions itself.
HybridMore like a thick client, but relies on servers for centralized services (database/file).

Virtualization

  • Virtualization — creating virtual devices (machine, server, router) by simulating their functions in software.
  • Roots — 1960s mainframe time-sharing; the hypervisor concept (IBM’s Don Skiba, 1969); VMware Virtual Platform (1999).
  • Virtual memory — an early application: hard-disk space extends main memory.
Hypervisor typeInstallation
Type 1 (“bare metal”)Installed directly on the hardware, taking the place of an operating system.
Type 2Installed on top of a native (host) operating system.

The hypervisor (virtual machine manager, VMM) is essentially the OS of the virtual environment: it allocates physical resources (RAM, disk, etc.) to each VM per its configuration. Network virtualization splits bandwidth into channels; storage virtualization makes many servers appear as one store; server virtualization makes many servers appear as one.

Cloud computing

  • Definition — access to resources/processing beyond the local firewall, via dedicated (typically virtualized) internet-based servers; shifts local processing to remote providers.
  • Why “cloud” — telephone diagrams drew a cloud for switching between central offices (POTS/PSTN); reused for the internet’s randomized switching.
  • Load balancing — CSPs often front applications with a load balancer to route a request to the fastest-responding server.

Cloud service delivery models (NIST) — the “what”

ModelDescription
SaaSApplications accessed on a CSP’s servers via the internet, typically through a browser; reduced licensing and no special hardware.
PaaSCustomized hardware/software environments for programming, testing, prototyping, and modeling.
IaaSServers, storage, networking, and virtualization delivered over the internet; typically pay-per-use.

Other “aaS” offerings: AIaaS, BaaS (backup), CaaS (a “full meal” of SaaS + PaaS + IaaS), DaaS (desktop), NaaS (network), UaaS (unified communications), VaaS (video-conferencing). The overall goal is Everything-as-a-Service (EaaS / XaaS).

Cloud service deployment models (NIST) — the “who”

ModelDescription
PrivateRestricted access (group/company); hosted in-house or via a CSP.
PublicEssentially a private cloud offered over a public network; membership is the constant.
HybridCombines public deployment with private-cloud restrictions.
Community (variation)Formed around shared areas of concern, action, or support.
