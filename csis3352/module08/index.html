S1 · LO1Define a Support Vector Machine▼

Explain what an SVM is, what it's mainly used for, and what kind of boundary it looks for.

  • What it is — a powerful supervised learning algorithm.
  • Main use — mainly classification; a regression version (SVR) also exists.
  • What it finds — the best possible boundary, a hyperplane, that separates two classes.
  • "Best" — the boundary with the maximum possible margin between the classes.
  • Where it shines — high-dimensional spaces, and data with a clear gap between classes.
S1 · LO2Explain the margin and why SVM maximizes it▼

Describe what the margin is and why SVM picks the boundary with the largest one.

  • Many possible lines — lots of lines could separate two classes.
  • SVM's choice — the one with the largest margin.
  • Margin defined — the distance between the boundary and the closest data points from each class.
  • Why wider is better — a wider margin generally means better generalization to new, unseen data.
S1 · LO3Explain support vectors and why they give SVM its name▼

Identify the support vectors and explain why only they matter for the boundary.

  • Support vectors — the data points closest to the decision boundary.
  • They set the boundary — they directly determine its position and orientation.
  • Everyone else — every other point could be removed without changing the boundary at all.
  • Efficiency — this makes SVM efficient at prediction time: only the support vectors matter.
S2 · LO4Contrast hard margin and soft margin▼

Explain the difference between the two and which one is used in practice.

  • Hard margin — requires perfect separation; no points allowed inside the margin.
  • Hard margin's limit — only works on perfectly separable data.
  • Soft margin — allows some points to be misclassified or inside the margin, to handle real, noisy data.
  • In practice — real-world data is rarely perfectly separable, so soft margin is used almost always.
S2 · LO5Explain how the C parameter controls the margin▼

Describe what a large C and a small C do to the margin and to mistakes.

  • C controls the soft margin — it sets how much misclassification is tolerated.
  • Large C — punishes misclassification heavily → narrower margin.
  • Small C — allows more flexibility → wider margin.
  • Tuning is tricky — picking a good C is listed as one of SVM's weaknesses.
S2 · LO6Explain the kernel trick▼

Describe the problem the kernel trick solves and how it solves it.

  • The problem — real data is often not linearly separable in its original form.
  • The trick — map the data into a higher-dimensional space, where a linear boundary can separate it.
  • Efficient — this happens mathematically, without ever directly computing the new coordinates.
  • Result — SVM can draw curved, complex boundaries back in the original space.
  • Example — an inner circle of one class inside an outer ring of the other can't be split by a line in 2D, but after mapping to 3D a flat plane separates them.
S2 · LO7Name and describe the four common kernel types▼

Match each kernel to when or how it's used.

  • Linear — no transformation; used when the data is already linearly separable.
  • Polynomial — creates curved boundaries using polynomial combinations of features.
  • RBF (Radial Basis Function) — the most popular choice for complex, nonlinear boundaries.
  • Sigmoid — behaves similarly to a neural network activation function.
  • Class notebook — uses kernel = 'linear'.
S3 · LO8Read the hyperplane equation and the classification rule▼

Explain each symbol in the decision boundary and how a point gets its class.

  • Decision boundary — w · x + b = 0.
  • w — the weight vector, perpendicular to the boundary.
  • x — the input features; b — the bias.
  • Classification rule — y = sign(w · x + b).
  • Meaning — which side of the boundary a point falls on determines its predicted class.
S3 · LO9Explain margin width and what training an SVM means▼

Connect the margin to ||w|| and describe training as an optimization problem.

  • Margin width — equals 2 / ||w||.
  • So — maximizing the margin means minimizing ||w||.
  • Training — solving an optimization problem to find the best w and b.
S3 · LO10List SVM's four strengths▼

Name each strength and say briefly why it helps.

  • High dimensions — effective in high-dimensional spaces.
  • Memory-efficient — only uses the support vectors.
  • Versatile — thanks to the different kernel choices.
  • Clear gaps — works well when there's a clear margin of separation.
S3 · LO11List SVM's four weaknesses▼

Name each weakness and connect at least one to the soft margin or kernels.

  • Slow training — slow to train on very large datasets.
  • Noise and overlap — performance drops with lots of noise or overlapping classes.
  • Tuning — choosing the right kernel and tuning C can be tricky.
  • No probabilities — doesn't directly output probabilities.
S3 · LO12Give SVM's real-world uses and key takeaways▼

  • Takeaways — finds the maximum-margin boundary · support vectors are the few points that define it · soft margins and kernels make it flexible for real, messy, non-linear data.

🧭 Part 1 — What SVM Is: Hyperplane, Margin & Support Vectors▼

What it is

  • Support Vector Machine (SVM) — a powerful supervised learning algorithm used mainly for classification. A version for regression, SVR, also exists.
  • What it finds — the best possible boundary, a hyperplane, that separates two classes.
  • "Best" — the boundary with the maximum possible margin between the classes.
  • Where it shines — high-dimensional spaces, and data where there's a clear gap between the classes.
⚠️ Not spelled out in the source: the lecture says "SVR" without expanding it. It stands for Support Vector Regression (scikit-learn documentation).
🧩 Study aid — "hyperplane" in plain words. With 2 features the boundary is a line; with 3 features it's a flat plane; with more features it's the same flat idea in more dimensions. "Hyperplane" is the general name. The class notebook has 2 features (Age, EstimatedSalary), so its hyperplane is a line.

The core idea: maximize the margin

  • Many lines could work — lots of lines separate the two classes above. SVM picks the one with the largest margin.
  • Margin — the distance between the boundary and the closest data points from each class (the gap between the dashed lines).
  • Why wider is better — a wider margin generally means better generalization to new, unseen data.

Support vectors: what gives SVM its name

  • Support vectors — the data points closest to the decision boundary (the ringed points above).
  • They set the boundary — they directly determine its position and orientation.
  • Everyone else — every other point could be removed without changing the boundary at all.
  • Efficient predictions — only the support vectors matter.
⚠️ Clarification beyond the lecture (scikit-learn documentation). The lecture's pictures show a clean gap, where the support vectors are just the points touching the dashed lines. With a soft margin (Part 2), points that sit inside the margin or on the wrong side also count as support vectors. That's why the class notebook's model ends up with 128 support vectors out of 300 training points (64 per class): the classes overlap. Keep the lecture's definition for exams.
💡 Study aid — memory hook. The support vectors are the tent poles. Pull out any other stake and the tent stays up; move a pole and the whole tent shifts.

⚠️ Not in the lecture: the ±1 lines in the drawing and the fact that sign returns −1 or +1. The usual math convention labels the classes −1 and +1 and puts the margin edges at w·x + b = ±1, which is where the 2 / ||w|| comes from. scikit-learn maps those back to your labels, so the notebook still prints 0 and 1. ("||w||" means the length of w.)
🧪 Study aid — the notebook's actual w and b (read from the trained model, not printed in class). After .fit, classifier.coef_ = w ≈ [1.60, 0.97] and classifier.intercept_ = b ≈ −0.77, in scaled units. So ||w|| ≈ 1.87 and the margin ≈ 2 / 1.87 ≈ 1.07. Age has the bigger weight, so it tilts the line more than salary does.
Check the single prediction: age 30, $87,000 scales to about (−0.81, 0.51). Then w·x + b ≈ 1.60(−0.81) + 0.97(0.51) − 0.77 ≈ −1.57 → negative → Class 0, matching the notebook's [0].
🧩 Study aid — the trailing underscore. In scikit-learn, attributes ending in _ (coef_, intercept_, support_vectors_, n_support_) only exist after .fit — they're what the object learned. Same idea as Java instance fields that a method fills in.
